Four items this week, and one question runs through all of them: where does your company's data sit once an AI tool has seen it? A new Meta model remembers what you tell it. An open-source agent company is selling the opposite, software that runs on your own servers. Salesforce is adding a voice agent that books service calls. Google priced a new model low, with a catch. Each ends with something to check this week.

**1. Meta's Muse Wonder remembers across conversations. Estimators should ask about bid-data retention first.**

Meta released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full on October 12. Coverage describes a feature Meta calls Active Memory, which keeps what a user shares over time, and a 1 million token context window. The API version is priced at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens, according to multiple outlets. Wonder Full is limited to Muse Code subscribers and is not served through the API. The benchmark claims are Meta's own and have not been independently verified. For an estimating shop, memory cuts both ways. It helps on a long bid where the model should remember the scope notes from last week. It also means sub quotes, unit pricing and margin assumptions may persist somewhere you have not looked. Before anyone uploads a bid, get the retention terms in writing: what is stored, for how long, who can see it, and how to delete it.

Source: [TechCrunch — Meta releases its first frontier model, Muse Wonder](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/12/meta-releases-its-first-frontier-model-muse-wonder) and [Pro Pakistani — Meta enters AI frontier with new Wonder models](https://propakistani.pk/2026/10/13/meta-enters-ai-frontier-with-new-wonder-models/). **2. Nous Research raised $90 million to sell a self-hosted AI agent. For a sub, "self-hosted" means you run it.**

Nous Research, maker of the open-source Hermes Agent, announced a reported $90 million Series B on October 7 at a $1.5 billion valuation. The valuation comes from Wall Street Journal reporting; the company's own note does not state one, and outlets differ on who led the round. The money funds "Hermes for Business," a commercial version meant to run on a company's own cloud or servers, with single sign-on, audit logs and workspace controls. Revenue figures, about $36 million annualized in mid-September with a goal above $100 million by year end, are reported, not audited. The pitch to a trade sub is control over bid data, pricing and payroll records. The catch is that someone has to install, secure and update it. If you have no IT person, price that labor before assuming self-hosting is cheaper or safer, and insist on audit logs for anything that touches pay applications, change orders or certified payroll.

Source: [Runtime Wire — Nous Research raises $90M to take its open-source Hermes agent into business](https://runtimewire.com/article/nous-research-90m-series-b-hermes-agent) and [Digital Today — Hermes raises Series B at $1.5 billion valuation](https://www.digitaltoday.co.kr/en/view/112594/open-source-ai-agent-hermes-raises-series-b-at-1-5-billion-valuation-targets-enterprises). **3. Salesforce's Winter '27 release adds a voice agent that books service appointments. Set the scheduling rules before it takes a call.**

Salesforce's Winter '27 materials describe Autonomous Scheduling with Agentforce Voice: an agent that checks real-time availability and picks a technician in the right territory while the customer is on the line, around the clock. Salesforce says it cuts a 15-minute booking call to seconds. That is a vendor figure, not a measured result from a contractor. One of our earlier reports said Agentforce would be switched on by default in eligible orgs; we could not confirm that in the Salesforce materials we found, so check your org's Setup pages and the release notes. For a mechanical or electrical shop running service in Salesforce, the work is not buying anything. It is deciding what the agent may promise: which service types it can book, emergency versus routine, overtime rules, and when it must hand off to a dispatcher. A voice agent that books a Friday 4 p.m. no-heat call into overtime without asking has made a pricing decision for you.

Source: [Salesforce — Winter 2027 product release announcement](https://salesforce.com/news/stories/winter-2027-product-release-announcement) and [Trailhead — Winter '27 service and field service highlights](https://trailhead.salesforce.com/content/learn/modules/winter-27-release-highlights/see-whats-new-service-field-service-it-hr-service-and-contact-center). **4. Gemini 4 Argon is priced at $2 and $10 per million tokens for now. Price your spec-review test at the later rate.**

Google's Gemini 4 Argon launches at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. A footnote in Google's announcement reportedly says $4 and $20 apply after an introductory period it does not define. Reports disagree on availability: some describe broad access, while at least one aggregator describes a more restricted rollout, so confirm what your account can reach before planning a test. Pricing pages we found are mostly third-party and we did not retrieve Google's page directly. The useful move for an estimator is a cost test, not a switch. Pick one spec section and one addendum, run a spec-review or scope-gap check, and record tokens used and the cost at both price points. If the answer only pencils out at $2, it does not pencil out.

Source: [eesel.ai — Gemini 4 Argon pricing: the $2/$10 promo, the $4/$20 bill](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gemini-4-argon-pricing) and [Four Week MBA — Gemini 4 Argon's $2/$10 price is half the later rate](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-gemini-4-argon-introductory-price-footnote-4-20/). Our take: [pricing a spec-review test](https://constructionaibrief.com/posts/2026-10-14-google-gemini-4-argon-intro-pricing-construction-spec-review-cost-test).
