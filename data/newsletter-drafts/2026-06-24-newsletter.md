**1. NIST says Surfside was failing for three weeks before it fell**

NIST's June 22 technical findings establish that the Champlain Towers South collapse began roughly three weeks before 98 people died on June 24, 2021. Two slab-column connections beneath the pool deck experienced punching-shear failures in early June. Those connections cracked, loads redistributed to adjacent columns already weakened by four decades of coastal corrosion and above-design loads from pool deck modifications, and the system progressively failed until the collapse.

The detection technology that would have flagged this — settlement tilt sensors, acoustic emission monitoring, electrochemical corrosion sensors — exists commercially today. The more immediate signal for GCs: NIST's final report is due by end of 2026 and will almost certainly drive code updates requiring more rigorous structural assessment before any alteration to aging concrete. Knowing the monitoring toolkit before those spec sections land is worth the prep time.

Two situations where establishing baseline sensor records before your scope starts makes sense: any scope that modifies dead or live load on a concrete structure with documented corrosion or spalling, and any project where the conditions report includes active corrosion or slab observations similar to what Champlain Towers' management had received for years. Baseline monitoring is also documentation that the structure was behaving normally when you left it — a useful thing to have if a dispute follows.

**2. Six national intelligence agencies: AI-enabled attacks on critical infrastructure are months away**

On June 22, the NSA, CISA, and counterparts from Australia, Canada, the UK, and New Zealand issued a joint statement: frontier AI models are now close to automating exploit development and phishing at a scale current defenses weren't built to handle. The agencies framed the timeline as months, not years.

The coverage has treated this as an IT story. The construction-specific exposure is the commissioning window. When a building's BAS, HVAC controls, access control, fire safety, and electrical monitoring pass from "being tested by the controls sub" to "accepted by the owner and connected to the enterprise network," there's typically a four-to-eight week period where temporary credentials haven't been rotated, multiple parties hold active remote access simultaneously, and OT networks are sometimes bridged to the general project network for commissioning data collection. None of that is negligence — it's the standard working condition near substantial completion.

Two spec changes that close most of the gap: require credential rotation and network isolation protocols as deliverables in Division 25 at substantial completion, and add a subcontract clause requiring written confirmation from the controls contractor that non-essential remote access is decommissioned by SC. If it's not in the spec, it's not in anyone's scope.

The White House executive order signed June 2 includes a July 2 deadline for a Treasury AI cybersecurity clearinghouse for critical infrastructure operators — hospitals, airports, utilities, data centers. If your firm is building for those owners, this is worth tracking before the owner's IT team asks you about it.

**3. NVIDIA built the infrastructure for a robot safety case. Here's what to ask vendors.**

NVIDIA announced Halos for Robotics on June 22 — the industry's first accredited inspection framework for physical AI systems working alongside workers. It's not a robot. It's the audit trail and hardware stack manufacturers use to prove their machine won't injure someone. The analogy: UL listing for industrial equipment.

Certification targets: IEC 61508 (electronic safety systems), ISO 13849 (machine safety), and ISO/IEC TR 5469 (AI safety). The inspection lab is accredited by the ANSI National Accreditation Board and recognized by TÜV Rheinland, TÜV SÜD, UL Solutions, and CertX — the same bodies that certify industrial equipment already used on construction projects. The first manufacturer pursuing Halos certification is Agility Robotics (Digit humanoid, commercially deployed at Amazon and GXO).

The caveat for construction: these certifications target controlled industrial environments with predictable paths and stable surfaces. They don't map directly to active construction sites, where terrain changes weekly and OSHA 1926 adds requirements that IEC 61508 alone doesn't address. But Halos changes what you can verify. A vendor that has gone through the inspection lab and is pursuing third-party certification has submitted its safety case to independent review — a materially different position than a vendor demo in a controlled showroom.

Three questions to add to any robot pilot agreement evaluation: (1) Which third-party certifier is reviewing your safety case, and what's the timeline? (2) Has your safety software been evaluated by an ANAB-accredited inspection lab? (3) What safety data exists from active construction deployments specifically — not warehouse deployments?

**4. CM/PM fees grew $8.8 billion in a single year. Data center work is why.**

ENR's 2026 Top 100 Professional Services Firms rankings (published June 22) put combined CM/PM-for-fee revenue at $36.9 billion in 2025 — up 31.2% from $28.1 billion the year before. Program management-for-fee firms grew 35.7%. International work grew 63.4% to $12.7 billion as hyperscalers extend the buildout to Europe and Asia-Pacific. ENR identifies the driver directly: telecommunications and data center work grew 31.3% for CM/PM firms between 2024 and 2025, and is up nearly 87% since 2023.

The constraint isn't backlog — it's headcount. Specialized PM talent with data center project history, digital project controls experience, and mission-critical commissioning familiarity is the scarce resource. If your firm has that credential stack, it's your competitive position for the next two to three years. If you don't, the gap is widening faster than most firms realize.
