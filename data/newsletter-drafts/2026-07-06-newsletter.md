Five things from the past few days, and three are really one story: the industry's own biggest spenders are hitting real limits and real costs on AI faster than the sales decks admit.

---

**1. Meta just told staff its AI agents haven't gotten better in four months — after $145 billion and a 10% headcount cut.**

At a July 2 town hall, Mark Zuckerberg told employees that Meta's agent development "hasn't really accelerated in the way we expected" over the prior four months, despite reassigning 7,000 people onto AI teams as part of a reorg that cut 10% of the workforce in May. Minutes later, AI chief Alexandr Wang told the same room an unreleased model had "caught up" to GPT-5.5 on unspecified benchmarks — a claim about a base model, not whether an agent finishes a multi-step task without a human catching the mistakes.

If the best-funded AI shop on earth can't hit its own agent timeline, that's the bar for the next vendor pitch promising an agent will draft your RFIs by Q3. Ask for a task-completion number from a pilot on your own documents, not the demo set, before planning headcount around it.

**2. Palantir's CEO called the AI industry's pricing model a "wealth tax" — and named Anthropic and OpenAI directly.**

In a heated CNBC interview, Alex Karp said enterprise AI vendors charge rising per-token fees while sitting on the customer data that improves their own models, calling the arrangement "effing insane." Palantir's stock rose more than 9% that day.

Karp's complaint is about token pricing, but the question applies to construction too: every GC and sub now runs project data — RFIs, submittals, drawings, cost history — through an AI feature bolted onto Procore, Autodesk, or Trimble. Before renewing at a higher tier, put it to the vendor in writing: what happens to our data, and does it improve anything beyond our own account?

Source: [Forbes — Alex Karp on the AI industry](https://www.forbes.com/sites/tylerroush/2026/07/01/palantir-billionaire-alex-karp-calls-ai-industry-effing-insane-in-heated-interview/)

**3. A Wall Street index tracking what companies pay for AI tokens just fell 20% from its May peak — the earliest possible read on your data-center backlog.**

Bloomberg reported July 3 that Silicon Data's LLM Token Expenditure Index — a measure of actual AI spend, not list prices — dropped about 20% from its May high after nearly doubling since December. The drop can mean two things: real demand cooling, or buyers switching to cheaper models for the same work. Nobody's calling it a verdict yet.

For a GC or MEP sub with data-center phases in backlog, that's the kind of number that shows up in financial data months before it shows up as a paused phase. Separate what's actually contracted and financed from what's only an announced future phase, and watch scope documents for hedge language like "subject to demand."

**4. Tesla capped employee AI spending at $200 a week. If your firm handed out Copilot or Claude seats with no ceiling, you're carrying the same exposure.**

Starting July 6, Tesla requires manager sign-off for any employee AI spending above $200 a week, after engineers reportedly burned through thousands of dollars in tokens weekly with no oversight. Uber, Meta, Amazon, and Walmart have made similar moves this year as usage-based billing turned every prompt into a real cost.

Construction firms sit on the same billing model at smaller scale: GitHub Copilot moved to metered "AI Credits" on June 1, and some accounts are seeing bills projected to jump from roughly $29 to $750 a month on agent-mode tasks. If nobody's checked your AI seats' usage dashboard since the pricing changed, that's this week's 20-minute task.

**5. A heat wave pushed data centers onto backup generators for the third time in 2026 — federally ordered, not a drill.**

On June 30, the Department of Energy ordered PJM-area facilities with 50+ MW of load, data centers included, to switch to backup generators within 15 minutes of a signal, as a record heat wave pushed grid demand past a 20-year peak — the third such order for PJM this year, and one of more than 40 issued nationally since May 2025.

For a GC or electrical sub pricing backup power on a data-center job, that changes the design assumption: genset and switchgear packages built for one annual test now need to prove a real 15-minute switchover, repeatedly, in a season — and fuel-supply contracts should assume more than one real activation a summer.

**Robotics spotlight: China's Unitree just IPO'd for $619 million — and Congress wants its robots off the US market.**

Unitree, maker of quadruped robots increasingly used for jobsite inspection at a fraction of Boston Dynamics' Spot ($2,800 versus roughly $74,500), won approval this week for a $619 million Shanghai IPO. Meanwhile the GUARD Act moving through Congress would apply the same Covered List mechanism that got DJI drones blocked from US sale to Chinese-made ground robots. Neither bill has had a floor vote — but it's the same fight the drone industry was having before DJI's ban landed. If you're evaluating a budget quadruped, ask where the hardware is built before standardizing a fleet around it.
