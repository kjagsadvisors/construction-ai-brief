Six items this week, and most of them are about who gets to vouch for an AI tool before you trust it with something that matters — a code-compliance call, a client's account, a product substitution. None of it requires your project to touch AI directly to change how you evaluate a vendor.

---

**1. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google have been quietly meeting since July to discuss building a shared body that would test and certify frontier AI models before release — and construction has run this exact playbook before with UL and ICC-ES.**

Nothing has been formed or agreed to yet; the three labs are still arguing over how much teeth it should have, from a FINRA-style voluntary review body to an FAA-style agency that can block a release outright. But UL and ICC-ES both started the same way — private, industry-funded testing bodies that became mandatory in practice once code officials, specifiers, and insurers started requiring the paperwork. If you're buying an AI tool that touches structural, code-compliance, or autonomous-equipment decisions, start asking the vendor today whether any independent party has evaluated it, even informally — that question costs nothing now and gives you a paper trail later.

**2. Anthropic launched Claude for Financial Advisors this week with source-locked data connectors, a per-action audit trail, and a hard human-approval gate before anything reaches a client — the first detailed blueprint a frontier lab has published for deploying AI inside a regulated profession with real liability.**

The product itself is for wealth managers, not contractors, but the architecture is the tell: data stays inside the custodian's system instead of getting copied to a third-party store, every AI-touched action logs against the record, and nothing client-facing goes out without a licensed human signing off. Construction carries the same kind of liability every time an RFI, a pay application, or a change order goes out under someone's signature. Before you let an AI tool touch one of those, ask your vendor the same four questions: does data stay in your system of record, is every action logged, is there a hard approval gate, and does it check output against your actual contract requirements — not just generate plausible-looking text.

**3. Jobsite-camera startup Buildots raised $130 million this week at a valuation near $1 billion, and its own customer list — Turner, JE Dunn, Digital Realty — shows exactly which projects are paying for AI progress tracking today.**

Buildots claims its computer-vision platform cuts project delays by half and saves an average of three months per job, based on its own customer data rather than an independent study, so ask for project-level backup before you take the headline number at face value. The money is chasing data center and other schedule-sensitive work specifically, where a month of delay costs real revenue on hardware that's losing value while it sits uninstalled. If you're running mission-critical or hospital work, a pilot is worth the helmet-camera hardware and subscription cost; on a single-building, non-critical job, it may not clear the bar yet.

**4. Marsh, the world's largest insurance broker, says it expects "explosive growth" in AI data center owners using their own captive insurers instead of traditional carriers — which changes who actually stands behind a subcontractor default or a builders-risk claim on the job.**

A captive is an insurer the owner sets up and controls itself, and Marsh alone already manages roughly 1,900 of them writing about $79 billion in premium. AGC and broker Gallagher both flag that many data center owners are already skipping subcontractor default insurance or bonding in favor of contractual indemnity, which pays out slower and weaker if a sub walks. If data center work is on your pipeline, ask upfront who owns the captive backing your coverage, whether SDI or bonding is still required, and get the claims-handling process in writing before you need it.

**5. Robotics spotlight: Agility Robotics unveiled Digit 5, a humanoid built to lift, sort, and tend machines next to people with no safety cage around it — and it's drawn more than $300 million in orders already.**

The robot lifts up to 50 pounds, runs 20+ hours a day on a 9-minute charge, and uses onboard sensing to stop or step aside when a person gets close instead of relying on fencing. It's aimed at structured factory and warehouse floors, not an active jobsite — but that's exactly what a modular or MEP prefab shop looks like, where Digit 5's target tasks (unloading pallets, gathering parts, tending machines) map directly onto material handling. Early access starts in the first half of 2027 at roughly $8,500 a month or $200,000 to buy, so this is a "start watching now" item for a prefab operation, not a this-quarter decision.

**6. Profound, a startup that helps brands get recommended inside AI chat answers, raised $180 million this week at a $1.8 billion valuation — proof that what ChatGPT or Perplexity says about a product is now a paid, targeted surface, not a neutral summary.**

The company doesn't sell to building-product manufacturers directly, but the category it's in — getting a product mentioned when someone asks an AI tool instead of typing a Google search — is the same layer manufacturers now compete in. A separate 2026 survey of roughly 800 architects found 64% already using AI tools daily. If you're leaning on an AI chat tool for a first-pass product suggestion or an "or equal" substitute, treat the answer like a sales rep's pitch: verify the code listing and pull the manufacturer's current cut sheet before it goes into a submittal.
