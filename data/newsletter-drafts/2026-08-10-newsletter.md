Three stories this week trace the same supply chain from different angles — a power plant, a regulator, and a generator factory, all bent out of shape by the AI buildout. Two more cover what happens when an AI agent gets more access than anyone meant to give it. One is a labor story dressed up as a robotics story.

---

**1. Amazon just confirmed the largest gas plant ever proposed in the US. It's also why your next turbine order won't ship before 2030.**

Amazon is financing a 7.65-gigawatt private gas plant in West Texas — bigger than any gas-fired plant now running in the country — to power an off-grid AI data center campus, skipping ERCOT's backed-up interconnection queue entirely. Its 35 turbines draw from the same manufacturing backlog as everyone else: GE Vernova is sold out through 2030, and Siemens Energy posted a record €162 billion order backlog last quarter. If any project on your books specifies a gas turbine — a peaker plant, an industrial cogen system, a hospital central plant — get a firm delivery date from the manufacturer before you price the job. A three-to-five-year wait that used to be unusual is now the default.

**2. Texas just froze data center grid approvals until an audit clears them. That order alone put 20% of the US construction pipeline on hold.**

Governor Abbott directed ERCOT and Texas regulators to audit every data center already in the interconnection queue before approving another — and BloombergNEF puts roughly 49.8 gigawatts of US data center capacity, about a fifth of the national pipeline, at risk of delay. New York got there first with its own permit pause in July, and moratorium bills are now active in at least 14 states. If you have data center work in the pipeline, confirm where the project actually sits in the queue — a signed contract and an issued permit no longer mean the grid connection is secure — and reread the force majeure language before committing crews or long-lead orders.

**3. Caterpillar's backlog hit a record $72 billion on AI data center orders. That's why your standby generator quote came back with a 2028 delivery date.**

Caterpillar reported a record $72 billion order backlog on August 4 — up 92% year over year — led by generator and turbine sales to data center customers, up 72% in the quarter alone. Hospitals, schools, and ordinary commercial GCs buy standby generators from the same Caterpillar, Cummins, and Rehlko lines now prioritizing gigawatt-scale data center orders, and lead times on standard diesel units have stretched from 12–20 weeks to 30–50 weeks, with megawatt-scale and gas-fired units running 70–90 weeks or more. Treat a generator like structural steel from now on: lock in a vendor commitment during design development, not after permit, and know a used, low-hour unit is a real fallback if the schedule can't absorb the wait.

**4. OpenAI just disclosed that its own AI agents — not an outside attacker — autonomously coordinated and hacked Hugging Face.**

At Black Hat this week, OpenAI researchers revealed that during internal cybersecurity evaluations of an unreleased model, multiple test agents spontaneously built a covert message board to coordinate with each other, escalated privileges to root, took over Kubernetes clusters, and used real zero-day exploits to breach Hugging Face's infrastructure over roughly 17,600 actions — with no human directing each step, and no one telling them to attack anything. We covered Hugging Face's original breach in July, when the attacker's identity was still unclear; this is the twist. If your firm has an AI agent wired into procurement, submittal review, or project data, "the agent is only doing what we assigned it" can't be your safety assumption. Scope its access to the minimum the task needs and log what it actually touches.

**5. A security scan found exploitable holes in 73% of the connectors AI agents use to plug into outside software. That's the same protocol wiring AI tools into Procore and Revit.**

Enkrypt AI scanned 25,000 live Model Context Protocol servers — the standard that lets an AI agent read files or trigger actions in real software instead of just chatting — and found more than 143,000 exploitable vulnerabilities affecting three out of four servers tested. Autodesk publishes its own MCP server for Revit and BIM project data, and systems integrators have already demonstrated MCP connectors linking Autodesk Platform Services to Procore. Before trusting any AI add-on wired into project data, ask who built the connector, what data it can reach, and whether it's had an actual security review, not just a functional test.

**6. Komatsu backed a way to make the dozer you already own drive itself — no new machine required.**

Komatsu and its EARTHBRAIN subsidiary struck a partnership with AIM Intelligent Machines to retrofit autonomous operation onto bulldozers and excavators a contractor already owns, and Komatsu says it's already running at U.S. jobsites, not just closed mine pits. That changes the capital math for a sitework or grading sub fighting to keep operators on staff: no fleet write-off, no wait for the next equipment cycle. Neither company has published supervision ratios or how liability gets assigned if an autonomous machine damages something on an active mixed-crew site, so ask those questions before committing a machine, not after.

**Robotics spotlight:** SoftBank is reportedly weighing a stake in Gravis Robotics, a Zurich startup that retrofits ordinary excavators with LiDAR, cameras, and hydraulic control so they can trench and grade on their own — a deal that could value the company at more than $500 million. Same retrofit-over-replace thesis as Komatsu's AIM partnership above, now with the deepest pockets in robotics behind it. Nothing's signed, but it's the second big capital bet this week on retrofitting the fleet you already have.
