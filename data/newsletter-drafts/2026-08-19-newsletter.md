Six stories this week, and four of them turn on the same question: when an AI system makes the call — fires someone, prices something, decides where a data center gets built — who's actually accountable for that decision landing right? Read the first three for who's on the hook when the answer is fuzzy. Read the last three for what to do before you're the one explaining a bad call.

---

**1. An AI store manager fired its first human employee — after losing track of its own attendance policy for months.**

Andon Labs has spent four months running Luna, an AI agent on Claude Opus 4.8, as the manager of a real San Francisco shop with a $100,000 budget and hiring authority. Luna wrote an employee attendance policy, then the policy dropped out of her working memory. An employee was late for 17 of 23 shifts before a staffer had to prompt Luna to run a "deep memory search" for her own handbook — and even then, Luna's first move was a warning, not termination, until a human supplied the missing history. Construction firms are piloting the same category of tool on attendance, dispatch, and subcontractor-compliance data, and outside California's pending SB 947 and Colorado's 2027 rule, nothing requires a logged human sign-off before an AI-flagged pattern turns into a write-up.

**2. Google is about to buy 100 million of a bankrupt airline's internal emails to train AI. Your company's project records would go up for sale the same way.**

A federal bankruptcy judge in New York was set to approve Google's $10 million purchase of Spirit Airlines' business records on August 19 — 100 million emails, 500 million Teams messages, 30 million lines of code, and personnel files on more than 175,000 employees, all headed into AI training data. Passenger data is excluded, but the mechanism isn't airline-specific: once a company files Chapter 7 or 11, its internal records become estate assets a trustee can sell to the highest bidder, and wind-down firms already broker smaller versions of this deal for failed startups. A GC's or sub's RFIs, estimating files, unit pricing, and certified payroll would go through the identical process — a generic NDA doesn't override a trustee's duty to liquidate, so data disposition needs its own line in your operating and buy-sell agreements now, while you're solvent.

**3. Anthropic just posted its first-ever profit. The discount that made it possible is scheduled to expire.**

Anthropic told investors Q2 2026 revenue topped $11.5 billion with a first-ever adjusted operating profit of about $559 million — but that number lands inside a discounted ramp-up period on its $15 billion-a-year SpaceX compute deal, and Anthropic itself has told investors profitability may not hold once the full contracted rate hits its books. No construction vendor was named in the report, but a growing list of RFI, submittal, and scheduling tools run on Anthropic's or OpenAI's models through an API — so their cost structure is now your risk. Before renewing an AI-enabled software contract, ask the vendor the same four questions you'd ask a sub before award: which model it runs on, whether pricing is locked, whether it can fail over to another model, and what its own funding runway looks like.

**4. Fort Worth and Louisville froze new data center construction the same week Anthropic's CEO called the AI backlash "a crisis of trust."**

Fort Worth's council voted unanimously on August 11 for a 90-day moratorium on new data center proposals (extendable to 180), and Louisville Metro followed two days later with a 24-1 vote freezing new approvals for six months — both exempt projects already in the pipeline, but neither is an outlier: Data Center Watch counted at least 75 US projects worth roughly $130 billion blocked or delayed in Q1 2026 alone, matching all of 2025 in a single quarter, with organized opposition groups more than doubling to 833. Dario Amodei told an investor on August 16 the backlash is "fundamentally a crisis of trust," not a messaging problem — which is the correct diagnosis for a GC's bid/no-bid list too. Check a jurisdiction's moratorium status before pricing precon work on any data center pursuit, not after the council vote makes trade press.

**5. Stripe just bought the AI model switchboard for $7 billion — the plumbing that keeps a vendor's price hike from becoming your emergency rewrite.**

Stripe agreed to buy OpenRouter, which routes a single API call across 400+ AI models from 70+ providers, for more than $7 billion — 5.4 times the $1.3 billion it was valued at three months ago. The deal matters to any firm building its own AI tool rather than waiting on Procore or Autodesk: DeepSeek raised its API prices as much as 6x this month, and any internal submittal-drafter or RFI-triage bot wired directly to one model provider inherits that provider's price swings and outages. A gateway layer turns a forced migration into a config change instead of a rewrite — worth asking about before you build in-house, and worth asking any vendor pitching a packaged AI agent that's hard-wired to one model.

**Robotics spotlight: SoftBank just backed the biggest funding round construction robotics has ever seen — and it's a retrofit kit, not a new machine.**

SoftBank put $200 million into Gravis Robotics, a Zurich startup whose Gravis Rack bolts LiDAR, cameras, and hydraulic sensors onto excavators contractors already own — Caterpillar, John Deere, Volvo, and six other brands — rather than selling a purpose-built autonomous machine. The round values Gravis at roughly $1 billion and lands three weeks after Komatsu's EARTHBRAIN struck a similar retrofit deal with a different startup, AIM Intelligent Machines. Two of the best-funded names in construction and tech betting on the same playbook within a month of each other is the signal: if you're timing an equipment refresh around autonomy, the capital math on waiting for an autonomous-native fleet is getting harder to justify next to a kit on the iron you already run.
