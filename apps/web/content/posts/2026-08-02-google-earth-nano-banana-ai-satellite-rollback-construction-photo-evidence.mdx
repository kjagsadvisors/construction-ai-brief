Google shipped a tool that let anyone generate a photorealistic image of any real address on Earth, then pulled it one day later after people started passing the fake scenes off as real. That's not a story about Google Earth. It's a preview of what happens the next time someone hands your project a "site photo" that isn't one.

## What did Google actually build and pull?

Google Earth's "Create Image" feature launched on the web on July 30, 2026. Users could zoom to a real location, tap "create image," type a prompt, and get a photorealistic scene generated by Google's Nano Banana 2 model — grounded in that spot's actual satellite, aerial, and 3D-mapping data rather than a blank canvas. Google marketed it directly at architects, urban planners, and real estate professionals: type "reimagine this empty lot as a vibrant retail district," and the tool renders it on top of the real parcel.

Google rolled the feature back on July 31, roughly a day after the global rollout. Its stated reason: "We've seen geospatial professionals using this feature for a range of useful purposes. However we've also seen people sharing screenshots of generated imagery that appear to violate our policies. So we're rolling back this feature in Google Earth while we work on implementing stronger guardrails." Brady Africk, a satellite-imagery analyst at the American Enterprise Institute, put the problem more bluntly: the tool made it "easier to generate convincing fake satellite imagery that can spread quickly online and mislead the public."

## Why does a one-day product rollback matter on a jobsite?

Because construction runs on the exact image types this tool touched, and Google built it for the exact professionals who'd use it on a project:

- **Site due diligence and feasibility studies.** Aerial and satellite pulls of a parcel are baseline documentation before a bid or a design starts.
- **Differing-site-conditions and storm-damage claims.** Before/after imagery is often the entire evidentiary record in a dispute.
- **Public hearings and BD pitches.** "Reimagine this lot as a retail district" is a rezoning-hearing rendering, word for word — Google named architects and urban planners as intended users.
- **Existing-conditions surveys** submitted alongside permit applications, where a reviewer is trusting that the image reflects the site as it actually sits today.

Every one of those depends on a reviewer, adjuster, or board member trusting that an image of a real address shows what's really there. Google's own tool proved that trust breaks down fast once a generated image leaves the app as a screenshot.

## Doesn't watermarking already solve this?

Not by itself, and this is the same gap Construction AI Brief flagged when [California's AI Transparency Act and the EU AI Act's transparency rules both became enforceable this week](https://constructionaibrief.com/posts/2026-08-02-eu-ai-act-california-sb942-disclosure-construction-lawsuit-gap). Google points to its SynthID watermark as an existing safeguard on AI-generated imagery. But SynthID is embedded in the file — and a screenshot, a printout, or a re-saved JPEG in a board packet routinely strips that kind of metadata. The Google Earth incident is exactly the failure mode: images left the tool as screenshots and started circulating with no indication they weren't real. A construction firm can't count on a watermark surviving the trip from someone else's AI tool into a claim file or a hearing exhibit.

## What should a GC or owner's rep do this week?

| Situation | What changes |
|---|---|
| Site concept renderings for a hearing or pitch | Label the file name and the presentation slide as a rendering — don't rely on embedded metadata a viewer will never see |
| Imagery submitted to support a claim | Keep the original file with intact timestamp and source metadata; a screenshot isn't evidence |
| A "satellite image" a counterparty produces in a dispute | Ask where it came from before treating it as fact — verifiable provenance, not visual realism, is now the bar |
| Marketing or BD renderings from a vendor tool | Ask whether the tool discloses AI generation in a way that survives a screenshot, not just in the original file |

None of this requires new software. It requires treating "looks like a real site photo" as no longer sufficient proof that it is one — a habit that costs nothing to start and gets expensive to skip once a fabricated image lands in a dispute file.
