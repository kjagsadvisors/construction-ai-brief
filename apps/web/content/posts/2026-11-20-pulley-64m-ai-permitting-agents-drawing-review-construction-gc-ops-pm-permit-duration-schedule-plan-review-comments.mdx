Pulley, a commercial permitting service that runs AI agents alongside licensed staff, raised $64 million this week and says it gets approvals up to 70% faster. Its customers include Amazon, Starbucks, JLL and Equinix, which means GCs doing retail rollouts, tenant improvements and data center work will start seeing it on owner-led jobs. The useful move for a PM is not to bank the faster date. It is to find out who is running permits, what the service actually checks, and what happens when comments come back.

## What did Pulley announce?

Per the company's [announcement as reported by CityBiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/936101/pulley-raises-64-million-to-expand-ai-powered-real-estate-development-platform/), the money comes in two pieces: a $41 million Series B led by Jump Capital with Catalyst Investors, and a $23 million Series A led by CRV and Susa Ventures. Pulley sells regulatory due diligence, permitting strategy, construction plan reviews and agency coordination. It plans to add site selection and AI drawing review for code compliance.

The numbers Pulley gives about itself:

| Claim | Figure |
|---|---|
| Commercial construction supported | More than $21 billion |
| Development time saved, combined | 11,750 weeks |
| Licensed professionals on staff | 75+, in all 50 states |
| Private plan review programs | 2,000+ cities and counties |
| Projects approved with no review comments | Over 40% |
| Drop in review comments, 2026 vs. 2025 | 65% |
| Faster approvals | "Up to 70%" |

All of these are self-reported. None comes with a per-jurisdiction breakdown, and "up to" sets a ceiling, not an average.

## Why should a GC care about the owner's permitting vendor?

Because the permit date sits on your schedule even when you don't pull the permit. On owner-led retail and data center programs, the owner or its developer often handles the building permit, and the GC builds a schedule around it. If the owner's team now expects a faster approval, that expectation can show up in your contract dates, your mobilization date and your release dates for long-lead gear.

Pulley's own figures show why caution makes sense. Over 40% approved with no comments also means most projects still get at least one round of comments. Each round sends the drawings back to the architect and engineers, not to the AI service.

## What should a PM ask at the precon meeting?

1. **Who owns the permit?** Is it the owner, the developer, Pulley, or you? Get it in writing, including who answers comments.
2. **Which review path?** Is the job going through the city's own plan check or a private plan review program? Durations and comment styles differ.
3. **What has the service done in this jurisdiction?** Ask for actual submittal-to-approval times there, not the national average.
4. **Can you see the tracker?** Ask for read access to permit status and comment letters so you aren't learning about a resubmittal from the owner's rep a week late.
5. **What does the AI drawing review check?** When that product ships, ask which codes it checks and who signs off. A pre-check that catches egress or accessibility issues is useful. It does not replace the engineer of record.

## Should you shorten the permit duration in your baseline?

Not yet. Keep the duration you would normally carry for that jurisdiction until the service has a track record there. Tie mobilization and permit-dependent releases, such as switchgear or rooftop units ordered for a specific permit set, to the actual approval date rather than the forecast. If the permit comes early, you gain float. If you bank the faster date and it slips, the delay claim fight starts on day one.

Permitting delays on large jobs are not new. Last month we covered [Finland halting site work at Google data center sites](/posts/2026-10-09-finland-halts-google-data-center-site-work-construction-preconstruction-permits) over a missing environmental assessment. That was an approvals problem, not slow paperwork, and a faster permitting service would not have fixed it.

## What are the limits?

- **Vendor-reported results.** Every speed and comment-rate figure comes from Pulley.
- **The jurisdiction still decides.** AI can prepare and pre-check a package. The building department or its approved reviewer issues the permit.
- **Design fixes stay with the design team.** Comment responses that change drawings still need the architect and engineers, on their schedule.
- **Drawing review is not shipping yet.** The announcement describes AI drawing review as a planned product, with no launch date.

**Takeaway:** On your next owner-led retail, TI or data center job, ask in precon whether the owner uses an AI permitting service. If they do, get tracker access and that service's history in the jurisdiction, and keep your normal permit duration in the baseline until the results justify a shorter one.
