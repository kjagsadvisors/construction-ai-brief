Google Cloud launched Gemini Enterprise for Legal on August 25, checking vendor and M&A contracts against a law firm's own risk playbook, flagging high-risk clauses, and citing the exact source language behind each flag. Strip out the word "legal" and that's the same pitch Document Crunch — now owned by Trimble — has been selling general contractors and subs on their subcontracts, spec books, and change orders for two years.

## What did Google actually launch?

Gemini Enterprise for Legal is a set of AI agents — Google's term for task-specific capabilities — covering contract lifecycle management, citation verification, brief drafting, data-subject-access-request handling, and regulatory monitoring. Early adopters named at launch include Cleary Gottlieb, Weil Gotshal, Freshfields, and Williams & Connolly. Cleary's managing partner, Jeff Karpf, said the firm is "embedding AI into our workflows in strategic and competitive ways" and that Gemini "can slot in with other daily work tools" to free up associate time.

For contracts specifically, the system reads vendor agreements, NDAs, and M&A documents against a firm's internal playbook, flags clauses that deviate from acceptable terms, and drafts proposed revisions — with citations that are supposed to trace back to the source document rather than a generated summary. It also connects, via Model Context Protocol, to the tools legal teams already run: iManage, NetDocuments, RelativityOne, Thomson Reuters, Docusign, and Harvey.

## Why should a GC or sub care about a law-firm product?

Because the mechanism is identical to what's already being sold into construction. Document Crunch — acquired by Trimble in a deal announced in April and set to close this year — reads subcontracts, prime contracts, and specs against a firm's risk standards, flags indemnification, pay-if-paid, liquidated-damages, and notice-deadline language, and cites the clause it's flagging back to the source page. That's precon and risk-management staff's version of exactly what Google just built for corporate lawyers.

The difference isn't the architecture. It's the playbook. Gemini Enterprise for Legal ships with no construction knowledge — no AIA or ConsensusDocs clause patterns, no state-by-state lien deadlines, no certified-payroll triggers. A law firm using it has to build that playbook itself, the same way a construction risk-management team would if they tried to point a generic contract-AI tool at a subcontract instead of buying a purpose-built one.

| | Gemini Enterprise for Legal | Document Crunch (Trimble) |
|---|---|---|
| Built for | Corporate law firms | GCs, subs, owners |
| Reads | Vendor agreements, NDAs, M&A docs | Subcontracts, prime contracts, specs, change orders |
| Flags against | Firm's own custom playbook | Construction risk standards + your playbook |
| Domain knowledge out of the box | None specific to construction | Pay-if-paid, notice deadlines, lien law, prevailing wage |
| Integrates with | iManage, NetDocuments, RelativityOne, Harvey, Thomson Reuters | Trimble Construction One ecosystem |
| Launch stage | New, August 2026 | In production on 10,000+ projects before the Trimble deal |

## Does this replace Document Crunch or tools like it?

Not now. But it's worth naming what the launch actually proves: a company with Google's engineering depth can stand up a working, cited, playbook-driven contract-review agent for an entire professional-services vertical in a single product launch — and it chose to plug into existing category leaders (Harvey among them) rather than build every layer itself. Construction AI Brief covered [Harvey's own move to post-train a proprietary legal model](/posts/2026-08-22-harvey-tenet-legal-ai-post-trained-model-construction-software-buy-vs-build) last week; Harvey is now also a named Gemini Enterprise for Legal integration partner. The category is consolidating fast in both directions — generalist platforms adding legal-specific layers, and legal-specific vendors deepening their own models — and construction's contract-risk tools are one clause library away from either company deciding the vertical is worth entering directly.

## What should a sub or GC actually do with this?

Two tests, and they apply regardless of which vendor is running the software. First: does every flagged clause link back to the exact line in your actual contract, not a paraphrase? Legal teams piloting Gemini Enterprise for Legal are being told to verify this before trusting it, and the same check applies to any construction contract-AI tool. Second: is the tool applying your organization's risk standards, or a generic template the vendor shipped? A tool that can't show its playbook is one you can't audit. If your current contract-review process — AI-assisted or not — can't pass both tests today, that's the gap to close before a bigger platform closes it for you.
