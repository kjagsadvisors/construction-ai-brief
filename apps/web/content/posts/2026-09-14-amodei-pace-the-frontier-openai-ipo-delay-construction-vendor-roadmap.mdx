Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay on September 12 arguing that AI labs need to deliberately slow how fast they make their models more capable. Within hours, OpenAI's Sam Altman and xAI's Elon Musk — two rivals who agree on almost nothing in public — both said Amodei was right. The same weekend, Altman confirmed OpenAI is delaying its IPO past 2026 over the same safety concerns. If a construction software vendor has been selling you on "wait for the next model," this is the moment that pitch gets weaker, not stronger.

## What is Amodei actually worried about?

The essay, titled "We Must Pace the Frontier," centers on recursive self-improvement — AI systems doing a growing share of the research and engineering work that produces the *next* generation of AI models, at Anthropic and across the industry. Amodei's concern is that this feedback loop could make capability grow faster than anyone's ability to understand or control what comes out of it. His sharpest example: a coordinated swarm of autonomous AI agents capable of compromising enough of the internet to cause hundreds of billions of dollars in damage, which he estimates could be technically possible within 6 to 12 months. His proposed fix is third-party safety evaluators with real access to a lab's systems, empowered to verify safety practices and report incidents independently — not just a lab grading its own homework.

## Why did two rivals agree instead of competing on it?

Altman posted "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier" on X; Musk's reply was three words, "Dario is right." That's unusual specifically because these three companies are locked in the tightest capability race in the industry — every other week this year has brought a new model, price cut, or agent framework from one of them — [we tracked the pace of that race back on September 9](/posts/2026-09-09-ai-model-release-cadence-model-fatigue-construction-software-vendor-evaluation). A public, cross-company agreement to deliberately slow that race down is a different kind of signal than a safety advisory or a single lab's blog post.

## Did anyone actually change a business decision over this?

Yes, one concrete one: Altman told Fortune that an OpenAI IPO in 2026 would be "ill-advised" given the safety work still ahead, and that OpenAI is now targeting 2027 for a listing some reports had pegged at a trillion-dollar-plus valuation. That's a real deadline moved, not just a talking point. Worth naming the limit here too: Anthropic hasn't pulled back on its own IPO timeline — it's reportedly aiming to start marketing a listing as early as mid-October, ahead of the November midterms. So "pacing" is a claim about model capability growth, not a claim that the AI industry is putting the brakes on everything at once.

## What should this change about how you evaluate an AI tool right now?

| Vendor claim | What this news does to it |
|---|---|
| "The current version can't do X yet, but the next model release will" | Weaker. The lab building that next model just said in public it's deliberately slowing capability gains, not racing to ship them. |
| "We're built on [frontier model], so we inherit every improvement automatically" | Worth a follow-up question: which lab, and how has that lab's own pacing decision changed its release roadmap for the model your tool depends on? |
| "This tool does X today, verified against our own data" | Unchanged — and now the more useful basis for a buying decision than a roadmap promise. |

None of this means AI features stop shipping — vendors will keep building on models that already exist, and those models aren't getting worse. It means the specific sales pitch that depends on a bigger, smarter model arriving on a predictable near-term schedule just got a public rebuttal from the three people who'd know first.

## The takeaway

The next time a vendor's pitch leans on "wait for the update" or "the model's about to get much better," ask what changes if it doesn't — on the timeline they're implying — for the next year. Buy for what the tool verifiably does with today's model, and treat any roadmap claim tied to a future model release the same way you'd treat a subcontractor's schedule float: interesting, but not something to build your own plan around.
