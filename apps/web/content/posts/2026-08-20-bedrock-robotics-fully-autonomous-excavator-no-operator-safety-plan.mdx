Bedrock Robotics says its retrofit kit is now running excavators on active jobsites with nobody in the cab, nobody piloting it remotely, and nobody standing by as a spotter. That's the construction-AI story this week that actually matters — not another funding round, but the first commercial claim that unsupervised heavy equipment is doing real earthwork on GC jobsites instead of a supervised pilot.

## What did Bedrock actually turn on?

On August 17, Bedrock Robotics announced its system had moved from supervised testing to full autonomy on customer sites. The retrofit kit — a sensor rack bolted to an excavator's cab roof, combining LiDAR, GPS, an inertial measurement unit, and eight HD cameras with an in-cab computer — installs the same day on machines ranging from 20 to 80 tons. A site manager still sets the plan: what to dig, how deep, where the material goes. The machine executes it without a human driving, remote-piloting, or spotting.

The safety control replacing the operator and the spotter is proximity-based: if a person or an object the system doesn't recognize gets too close, the excavator stops on its own and waits until the area clears. There's no fenced exclusion zone described in the announcement — the machine is meant to work in normal site conditions alongside other equipment and crews.

## Where is this actually running?

| Site | GC / partner | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Water treatment facility, Nevada | Sundt Construction | Now running fully autonomous |
| Manufacturing facility, 130 acres, Phoenix, AZ | Sundt Construction | Earlier phase — 65,000+ cubic yards moved under supervised autonomy |
| Civil sitework | Zachry Construction | 1.2 million cubic yards, now fully autonomous |
| Earthwork | Champion Site Prep, Texas | Multi-million cubic yards, now fully autonomous |

Sundt and Zachry are both large self-performing GCs with heavy civil capability, not small pilot customers — which is the more telling detail than the technology claim itself. The Phoenix site was, as of earlier this year, described as the industry's largest known supervised-autonomy deployment; it loaded human-driven dump trucks the same way a human operator would. The August update is these teams (and others) graduating that same approach to no human oversight in the loop at all.

## What does "no operator" actually change on your site?

Every existing site safety plan assumes a person is in that seat — a competent operator, backed by exclusion zones and a spotter when visibility is limited. Bedrock's model swaps all of that for automated proximity detection controlled by software the crew didn't write and can't inspect on the fly. If you're a GC or civil sub whose safety director is writing a site-specific safety plan or JHA for a job where this kind of equipment shows up — your own fleet or a sub's — that plan needs an explicit section for autonomous equipment: what the stop-distance behavior actually is, who has authority to shut it down, and how it's logged when it does. "The vendor says it stops automatically" is not a line item OSHA documentation or your insurance broker will accept without more detail.

That last point is worth raising with your broker directly, before you're the one explaining an incident involving a machine with no operator of record. Nobody has litigated that yet at scale; you don't want your project to be the first.

## Should a sitework sub take this seriously yet, or wait?

Three autonomous-excavator retrofit stories have broken in the past two weeks: Komatsu partnering with AIM Intelligent Machines on dozers, SoftBank putting $200 million into [Gravis Robotics](/posts/2026-08-18-gravis-robotics-softbank-excavator-retrofit-funding), and now Bedrock moving from supervised to unsupervised on named GC jobsites. The funding and partnership stories tell you where capital is betting. This one tells you the technology is already doing production work without a person controlling it in real time — a threshold the others haven't crossed publicly yet. Bedrock says dozers, dump trucks, and loaders are next, working toward coordinated autonomous fleets rather than single retrofit machines.

If you self-perform excavation, the immediate move isn't to buy a kit — it's to get your safety director and your insurance broker into the same conversation before a GC bids a job assuming a supervised-autonomy safety plan works for equipment that no longer has anyone supervising it in person.
