Google is testing an AI agent that calls businesses for you, states out loud that it's an AI, and records the line — and that disclosure script is the part worth copying before a GC or sub points anything similar at a supplier.

## What did Google actually ship?

On September 24, Google began testing "Call for Me," a Gemini feature that places outbound phone calls on a user's behalf. A user can ask Gemini to call a business to check hours, get a price on a part, or book, confirm, or change a reservation. When Gemini dials, it opens by identifying itself as an AI assistant, names the person it's calling for, and states that the call is being recorded. The user sees a live transcript scroll on their screen for the whole call and can jump in and take over speaking at any point.

The test is narrow. It's limited to US-based Pixel 11 owners who pay for a Gemini subscription and have also opted into the separate Phone by Google public beta — not a general rollout, and Google hasn't said when that changes.

## Why does a consumer errand feature matter to a construction office?

Strip out the consumer framing — "check if the hardware store has this in stock," "move my dinner reservation" — and the mechanics are the exact shape of calls a GC's project engineer or a sub's office admin already makes every day: calling a supplier to confirm a lead time, calling the building department to check an inspection slot, calling a sub to chase a schedule update. Those are outbound, repetitive, low-judgment calls — precisely the kind of task the "voice/agent assistant" wave has been aimed at for field-to-office reporting and dispatch all year.

What's new isn't the idea of a voice agent making calls. It's that Google had to ship an answer to the disclosure problem first: say you're an AI, say who you're calling for, say the line is recorded. Any construction company building or buying a similar tool inherits that same problem, and most haven't faced it yet — voice AI in construction has mostly stayed inside the company (daily logs, dictation, internal dispatch), not calling outside vendors who never agreed to talk to a bot.

## Does call recording law actually apply here?

It does, and it's messier than most back offices realize. US call recording law splits by state:

| Consent model | What it requires | Example states |
|---|---|---|
| One-party consent | Only the person recording has to agree | Most states, including Texas, New York, Ohio |
| All-party consent | Everyone on the call has to be told and agree | California, Florida, Pennsylvania, Illinois, Washington |

A GC or a multi-state mechanical sub calling suppliers or subs across state lines isn't calling from a single-state bubble. If any leg touches an all-party-consent state, the safer position is to disclose the recording every time — which is exactly what Google's script does by default, not as an optional setting.

## What should a contractor do with this before building anything?

Nothing here says a GC or sub needs to rush out and deploy an AI calling agent this week. It says: if that's on the roadmap — even informally, even as "let's try an AI to chase suppliers for lead times" — copy the disclosure pattern before the first real call goes out, not after a supplier complains about being recorded without warning. Two concrete steps:

1. **Map which states your outbound calls touch** — supplier HQs, permit offices, sub offices — and check whether any require all-party consent (Thoughtly and Dialzara both publish current state-by-state trackers).
2. **Script the disclosure at the top of every call**, whether the tool is a Gemini-style consumer feature, a construction-specific voice platform, or something built in-house: state it's an AI, state who it's calling for, state the call is recorded — before asking the first real question.

That's not a workflow win yet. It's the compliance floor under whatever workflow win comes next.
