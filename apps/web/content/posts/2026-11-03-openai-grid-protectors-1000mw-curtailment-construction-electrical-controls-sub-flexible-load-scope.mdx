OpenAI says its future data center campuses will cut their power use when the local grid is stressed, and the first one, in Georgia, can be throttled by up to 1,000 megawatts. For electrical and controls subs, that means the load-shed system is now part of the building, and someone has to design, install and commission it.

## What did OpenAI announce?

The Grid Protectors program, announced Nov. 2, commits OpenAI-developed US campuses to reduce consumption at times of high grid demand. According to [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-grid-protectors-program-demand-response-georgia), each campus and its utility agree on how much power can be curtailed, how fast, and for how long. Some sites may also feed power to the grid from on-site generators or batteries. OpenAI says it will pay the full cost of the power lines and service each campus needs.

The Georgia pilot is the planned campus in Effingham County. [Georgia Power's announcement](https://southerncompany.mediaroom.com/2026-07-22-Georgia-Power-to-serve-OpenAI-project-in-Effingham-County) says the facility is expected to need about 3,200 MW and that OpenAI agreed to up to 1,000 MW of flexible demand response under a 25-year agreement. The announcement gave no construction start date. No other sites have been named.

## What does this change on the construction side?

A building that must drop a third of its load on command has to be built to do that safely. Likely touchpoints, depending on the owner's design:

| Scope item | Why curtailment matters |
|---|---|
| Controls and SCADA integration | Utility signals must reach the compute and cooling systems and be tested end to end |
| Switchgear and metering | Staged load-shed needs breakers, relays and revenue-grade metering that match the utility agreement |
| On-site batteries or generators | If a site exports power, interconnection and protection settings become a commissioning item |
| Mechanical systems | Cooling must ramp down and back up without tripping equipment |
| Commissioning | Curtailment tests add integrated-systems testing days, and they need the utility's participation |

Most of this is inference from how demand response works, not published OpenAI specifications. The company has not released site designs.

## Should a sub change how it bids?

Not yet, but ask questions before the drawings arrive. Four worth putting in writing:

1. Is the load-shed controls package in the electrical scope, the controls scope, or owner-furnished?
2. Who owns commissioning of the utility signal path, and what happens to your schedule if the utility isn't available to witness tests?
3. Are batteries or generators in the base design, and who handles interconnection studies and relay settings?
4. Which party carries the risk if curtailment testing finds a design problem after energization?

## Is this a real shift or a talking point?

Some of both. Georgia Power says flexibility could let it build fewer new generation resources, and the Next Web reports the utility expects savings of about $950 million a year from 2029 from deals like this. OpenAI also concedes that flexibility cannot replace new plants and transmission. Terms will vary by utility, so a bid for one campus won't carry to the next.

The practical takeaway: when an AI campus RFP lands, read the utility agreement summary alongside the specs. If curtailment is in it, price the controls, testing and commissioning time now, rather than discovering them as a change order.
