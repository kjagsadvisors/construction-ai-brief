Microsoft and Meta reported earnings within hours of each other on July 29, and both raised how much they're spending on AI infrastructure in 2026. Wall Street sent Microsoft's stock up roughly 7-8% after hours and knocked Meta's down nearly 8% [2][4]. If your firm builds or bids data center work, that split — not the raw spending totals — is the number to sit with, because it's already changing how these projects get financed and who your contract is actually with.

## What happened on the earnings calls?

Microsoft's fiscal Q4 revenue hit $90.01 billion, up 18% year-over-year and about $2.4 billion ahead of estimates, with adjusted earnings beating too [1][2]. Quarterly capex hit roughly $41 billion including finance leases, up 69% year-over-year, pushing full fiscal 2026 property-and-equipment spending to about $116 billion — up nearly 80% from $64.55 billion the prior year [1][2]. Its CFO pointed to continued capex growth into fiscal 2027 on strong "demand signals" [2].

Meta's numbers told a different story. Earnings per share of $6.18 missed the $7.14 consensus, and free cash flow fell to $784 million on $31 billion in quarterly capex [3][4]. Meta also raised its full-year 2026 capex guidance to $130-145 billion, on the same call [3][4]. Revenue technically beat estimates — the stock dropped anyway, on the cash-flow squeeze [4].

## Why did the same kind of news get opposite reactions?

Investors are pricing in whether the spending is already paying for itself. Microsoft's cloud growth gives them a reason to believe the capex is buying something that already shows up on the income statement. Meta is spending at a similar scale on a slower, less proven payback story, and this quarter's free cash flow made that gap visible [3][4]. Same input — a bigger 2026 AI infrastructure budget — read as bullish from one company and as a red flag from the other.

## What did Meta do about that pressure, and why does it matter to a GC?

Two days before its earnings call, Meta announced a joint venture with BlackRock to build a $14 billion, 1-gigawatt data center in El Paso, Texas [5][6]. BlackRock-managed funds hold 80% of the venture; Meta holds 20%. Meta put in land and construction-in-progress assets worth about $2.3 billion and collected a $1 billion payout to balance the ownership math. BlackRock is funding its stake with roughly $4.9 billion in cash plus about $12.5 billion in debt. The facility is expected online in 2028, with Meta as the sole initial tenant [5][6].

That structure is the point — it moves a $14 billion project off Meta's balance sheet while still securing the compute it needs, which is exactly the kind of deal a company under investor pressure over capex has an incentive to keep doing. For contractors, the entity signing your GMP contract and sitting on the other side of a change order isn't necessarily "Meta" — it's a newly formed, debt-financed joint venture where the hyperscaler is a minority owner and tenant. That changes what you check before you bid:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is the owner of record a hyperscaler entity or a project-finance joint venture? | Determines whose balance sheet is actually backing payment |
| How much of the project's capital stack is debt versus equity? | Project debt has priority in a slowdown or default; equity absorbs the hit first — know which one you're behind |
| Who has approval authority on change orders and pay apps — the JV, the asset manager, or the tenant? | JV governance structures can slow decisions that used to move at one company's pace |
| Does your state's mechanic's lien law reach a special-purpose JV the same way it reaches a corporate parent? | Lien priority against a project-financed entity isn't automatically the same as against Meta Platforms Inc. |

## What about Microsoft's building spec?

Microsoft also disclosed a quieter change: starting fiscal 2027, it's extending the assumed useful life of its data center and office buildings from 15 years to 25 years [1][2]. That's an accounting decision, not a design mandate — but an owner underwriting 25 years of service life is making a different bet than one planning a 15-year write-off. On an RFP or design-assist scope, ask directly: what service life is the owner assuming, and does the mechanical, electrical, and structural spec actually support it?

## The honest limit here

A stock drop doesn't void a contract, and a joint-venture structure isn't automatically higher risk — BlackRock and Meta are both investment-grade counterparties, and plenty of well-financed projects run through SPVs for tax and financing reasons that have nothing to do with distress. The point isn't to avoid this work; it's that "we're building for [big tech name]" stopped being a complete answer to "who's actually paying us" once these deals started running through minority-owned joint ventures with their own debt. [We flagged a version of this same counterparty question when Nvidia moved to guarantee $250 billion of OpenAI's financing](https://constructionaibrief.com/posts/2026-07-27-nvidia-openai-ohio-data-center-guarantee-contractor-payment-risk) — get the capital stack in writing before you get to the schedule.
