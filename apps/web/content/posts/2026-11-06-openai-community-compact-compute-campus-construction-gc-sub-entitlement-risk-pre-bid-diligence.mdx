OpenAI says its future data center campuses will pay their own power and grid-upgrade costs, stop requiring NDAs from local officials, and publish site plans. None of that binds a contractor, but it hands GCs and subs better questions to ask before pricing a job.

## What did OpenAI announce?

On Nov. 5, OpenAI released two documents: a National Community Compact and a Compute Campus Policy Blueprint. According to [RuntimeWire](https://runtimewire.com/article/openai-pay-power-bills-drop-ndas-data-center-compact), future campuses OpenAI owns and operates would cover their full electricity and infrastructure costs, and public officials would not be required to sign NDAs. [Android Headlines](https://www.androidheadlines.com/2026/11/openai-data-center-policy-community-compact.html) reports OpenAI also plans to publish site plans, noise studies and expected water demand, and cites local pledges in Effingham County, Georgia and Pike County, Ohio.

The blueprint is aimed at lawmakers. [Rolling Out](https://rollingout.com/2026/11/05/openai-data-centers-backlash-blueprint/) and [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/artificial-intelligence/articles/2546822) tie the release to the midterms, where coverage says voters in several states backed data center restrictions. We could not read OpenAI's own documents, so the details here come from news coverage. Reports did not give enforcement terms.

## Why should a GC or sub care about a company pledge?

Because the things OpenAI is promising to disclose are the things that sink schedules. A data center job usually dies or slips on approvals, utility capacity, or who pays for the grid work, not on the building design.

| Commitment (as reported) | What it gives you on a bid |
|---|---|
| Campus pays its own power and grid costs | A clearer answer on who funds substation and line work, which drives energization dates |
| Public site plans, noise and water data | Documents you can check against your scope, logistics and noise limits |
| No NDAs for local officials | Fewer surprises when a council learns the real project size late |
| Blueprint asks utilities to protect other customers if a project falls through | Pressure to settle who carries stranded grid costs, a payment-security question for you |

## What should estimators and PMs ask before pricing?

Treat the compact as a prompt, not a promise. Before you carry a number on an OpenAI-linked campus, or any similar one:

1. **Which commitments apply to this site?** Reporting says the compact covers campuses OpenAI owns and operates. A leased or partner-built site may not be covered.
2. **Is approval final?** Ask for the status of zoning, utility service agreements and any pending ballot or council action.
3. **Who funds the grid work, and when is power promised?** Get the energization date in writing and tie your schedule relief to it.
4. **Are site plans and noise studies public?** Compare them with your hours, laydown and truck routes.
5. **What happens to your costs if the project stops?** Look at termination and stored-material language. Our earlier piece on [data center delay clauses](/posts/2026-10-26-ai-data-center-5m-daily-late-penalties-construction-sub-delay-clauses-ld-caps) covers where subs get exposed.

## What is still unclear?

Plenty. The pledge is voluntary. Coverage gave no enforcement mechanism and no dollar figures for the power commitment. "Full cost" of grid upgrades is a company statement, and utilities and regulators will decide the actual tariff terms. The blueprint is a proposal to legislators and may change. For a sub, the practical read is simple: a public commitment is useful leverage in a bid conversation, but only a contract clause or a utility agreement protects your schedule and payment.

## The takeaway

Add a one-page "approvals and power" section to your data center bid review: approval status, who pays for the grid work, the promised energization date, and what your contract says if the owner stops. If the owner cites the compact, ask for the site-specific agreement that backs it up.
