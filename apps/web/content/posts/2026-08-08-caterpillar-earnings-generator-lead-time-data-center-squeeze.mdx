Caterpillar reported a record $72 billion order backlog on August 4 — up 92% from a year ago — and the company said the growth is led by generators and turbines going into AI data centers. That's not just a Wall Street story. The same Caterpillar, Cummins, and Rehlko factories building that backlog are the ones a hospital, a school, or an ordinary commercial GC calls when a project needs a standby generator. Right now, they're behind a very long line.

## What did Caterpillar actually report?

Sales hit $20.5 billion for the quarter, up 24% year over year and the first time the company has cleared $20 billion in a single quarter. Construction Industries — the segment that sells the excavators, dozers, and loaders most GCs actually rent or buy — grew 35% to $8.3 billion, with North American construction demand up 50%. But the standout was Power & Energy: sales of large generator sets and turbines to data center customers grew 72%. CEO Joe Creed told analysts on the earnings call that the $72 billion backlog is up $9 billion just since the prior quarter, that 59% of it is scheduled to deliver within 12 months, and that some Power & Energy customers are already placing orders for 2030.

## Why are generator lead times stretching out to 2028 and beyond?

Hyperscale data center developers have gotten tired of waiting on utility grid interconnection queues that can run years, so many are buying their own on-site gas generators and turbines to power the building directly while the grid connection catches up. That demand is enormous and it pays well, so manufacturers are filling those order books first. Rehlko (formerly Kohler) said it signed 1.8 gigawatts of new purchase agreements for data center backup generators in just 60 days. Caterpillar is bringing back 1.5 gigawatts of shelved manufacturing capacity for its 10-megawatt gas engine platform, with shipments not starting until the fourth quarter of this year. Everyone else buying a generator — for a hospital, a school, a water treatment plant, a distribution center — is in the same queue.

## How much has this actually moved lead times?

| | Before the AI data center buildout | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Standard diesel standby (500 kW–2,000 kW) | Typically 12–20 weeks | 30–50 weeks |
| Megawatt-scale / gas-fired units | Under a year | 70–90+ weeks |
| Large gas reciprocating engines & turbines | Under 18 months | Into 2028–2030 |

Those figures come from industry reporting on the generator market rather than a single source, but they're consistent with what Caterpillar itself disclosed about its own order book.

## What should an estimator or precon lead do with this?

1. **Ask about standby power the moment a project brief includes a generator, not during equipment procurement.** If the design calls for emergency or standby generation of any real size, treat the generator like structural steel or switchgear: a long-lead item that needs a vendor commitment locked in during design development, not after permit.
2. **Price the lead time into the schedule, not just the equipment cost.** A generator quote that comes back with a 2027 or 2028 delivery date isn't a vendor being difficult — it's the current market. Build float around it or flag it to the owner early, before it becomes the reason a certificate of occupancy slips.
3. **Know the used-equipment option exists, and price it as a real alternative.** Buyers who can't absorb a year-plus wait are turning to proven low-hour used units at a fraction of new-unit cost. It won't fit every spec, but for a schedule-critical project it's worth quoting alongside new.

The honest read: this squeeze isn't a temporary blip that clears once a few big data center orders ship. Caterpillar's own backlog shows customers booking generator capacity through 2030, and the company is still racing to add manufacturing capacity rather than sitting on excess. Until that backlog breaks, a generator is one of the highest-risk line items on any bid that includes one — whether or not the project has anything to do with AI.
