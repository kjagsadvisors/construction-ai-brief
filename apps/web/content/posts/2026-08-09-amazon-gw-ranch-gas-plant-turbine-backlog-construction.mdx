Amazon confirmed on August 7 that it's financing a 7.65-gigawatt gas power plant in Pecos County, Texas — bigger than any gas-fired plant now running in the United States — to power a new AI data center campus that will operate off the grid entirely. That single project, alongside dozens like it, is a big reason gas turbine manufacturers are now sold out for the rest of the decade. If your business involves ordering a gas turbine for anything — a peaking plant, an industrial cogen system, a hospital central plant — that backlog is now your schedule risk too.

## What is Amazon actually building?

The plant, known as GW Ranch and developed by Pacifico Energy, would run 35 gas turbines generating a combined 7.65 gigawatts, plus 1.8 gigawatts of battery storage and 750 megawatts of on-site solar. Cleanview, a market-intelligence firm, first tied the project to Amazon by matching satellite imagery of land-clearing activity to three data center building permits Amazon filed for the same site — each building just over 189,000 square feet, costing roughly $300 million, with construction running August through December 2026. Amazon has since confirmed its role in financing the power plant. The company is targeting first power by the first quarter of 2027.

## Why build a private gas plant instead of just connecting to the grid?

Because the alternative is a wait with no fixed end date. ERCOT's interconnection queue is backed up with roughly 474 gigawatts of pending requests, about 90% of it from data centers — and Texas just ordered a full audit of that entire queue before approving anything new, a freeze BloombergNEF says puts a fifth of the national data center construction pipeline at risk of delay. Amazon's answer is to skip the queue: build the generation on-site, run the campus off-grid at first, and connect to ERCOT later if and when the timing works. The plant's permitted emissions ceiling — up to 33 million metric tons of greenhouse gases a year, which would make it the single largest pollution source in the country — is the tradeoff for that speed.

## Where does the construction backlog actually land?

Not just in Texas, and not just at data centers. Gas turbines for large power plants come from three manufacturers — GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Baker Hughes — and all three are running record backlogs on the same driver:

| Manufacturer | Backlog (Q2 2026) | Trend |
|---|---|---|
| GE Vernova | 116 GW, up from 100 GW in Q1 | Sold out through 2030 |
| Siemens Energy | €162 billion (~$187B), record | €17.9B in new orders this quarter alone |
| Baker Hughes | Record quarter for gas turbine orders | Cited data centers as primary driver |

Cleanview Research separately counts 46 data centers across the US now planning roughly 56 gigawatts of their own on-site power generation, about three-quarters of it gas-fired — GW Ranch is the largest single example, not an outlier. Every one of those projects is placing turbine orders against the same manufacturing capacity a utility, an industrial plant, or a hospital system would draw from for a conventional power project.

## What does this mean for a GC or owner planning a power project?

Two separate things, and both are concrete:

1. **If you're chasing hyperscale data center work,** expect more of it to come bundled with a private power plant rather than a straightforward utility tie-in. That's a different scope entirely — turbine erection, gas pipeline tie-ins, battery and solar installation alongside the data center buildings — built on a different permitting track (state environmental and air-quality review, not a utility interconnection agreement) and a different risk profile than the construction most GCs are used to pricing.
2. **If you have any project on the books that specifies a gas turbine** — a peaker plant, an industrial cogeneration system, a campus or hospital central plant — get a firm delivery date from the manufacturer before you commit to a schedule. A wait that would have been unusual two years ago is now the default, and it has nothing to do with your project's size or priority. It's about how much of the national turbine supply data centers like this one have already claimed.

The honest read: Amazon didn't build its way around the grid bottleneck — it built a bigger one, just further up the supply chain. The interconnection queue was never the only place a data center project could get stuck. Now the turbine order is too, and that risk doesn't stay contained to data centers.
