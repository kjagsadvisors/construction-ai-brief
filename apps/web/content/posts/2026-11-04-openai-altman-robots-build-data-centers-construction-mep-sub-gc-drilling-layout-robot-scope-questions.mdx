OpenAI's robots are not on any jobsite yet, but the data center trade they're aimed at already has working robots doing one narrow task: drilling concrete. If you sub on hyperscale work, the useful question isn't whether Altman's vision comes true. It's which of your scopes a robot can already touch and who gets the savings.

## What did Altman actually say?

On the Cheeky Pint podcast, released Oct. 29, Altman said OpenAI will point its robotics program at building data centers, and called self-replicating data centers built by AI-controlled robots "within grasp," according to [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/sam-altman-openai-robots-self-replicating-data-centres-cheeky-pint). He tied it to making compute cheaper, with robots also building turbines and solar panels.

The host asked whether permits and parts, rather than labor, are what slow data centers down. Altman's answer was that robots could make more of those parts over "a long period of time." That is a long way from a crew on a deck. [CryptoBriefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/openai-robots-build-data-centers-altman/) notes "within grasp" expresses ambition, not a delivered capability, and that a humanoid demo isn't expected until 2027.

## What robots already work on these sites?

The nearest real example is the DEWALT robot built on August Robotics' platform. It drills holes in concrete for server rack stops and for the legs that support overhead MEP systems. DEWALT announced [commercial availability on July 9](https://newsroom.stanleyblackanddecker.com/2026-07-09-Revolutionizing-Construction-Introducing-the-Worlds-First-Fleet-Capable-Downward-Drilling-Robot,-Now-Commercially-Available-to-Accelerate-Data-Center-Construction).

Vendor-reported figures, per [BD+C](https://www.bdcnetwork.com/aec-tech/news/55357203/a-new-downward-drilling-robot-helps-speed-data-center-construction):

| Claim | Reported figure |
|---|---|
| Speed vs. traditional drilling | Up to 10x faster |
| Accuracy | 99.97% across 90,000+ holes (hyperscaler pilot) |
| Schedule effect | 80 weeks saved across 10 projects |
| Cost per hole (one project) | $65 down to $20 |

All of that comes from DEWALT, and the pilot customer is unnamed. Treat it as a claim to test on your own deck, not a benchmark.

## What does this mean for an MEP sub or GC?

Drilling for anchors and supports sits at the front of the MEP sequence. If a robot finishes that work in days, trades behind it lose their float. Three people feel it:

1. **Estimators:** Price per hole and crew-hours change. Ask whether the GC expects a credit if the robot is furnished by the owner or GC.
2. **PMs:** Look-ahead schedules compress. Confirm inspection holds and layout approvals can keep pace.
3. **Foremen:** Hangers and trapeze supports show up against holes you didn't lay out. Check that drawings match the as-drilled locations.

## Should a mid-size sub act now?

Don't buy a robot. Do add four bid questions on your next data center job:

- Who furnishes the robot and who owns the layout data?
- Is as-drilled data shared with the coordination model?
- Who pays to correct an out-of-tolerance or missed hole?
- Does the schedule assume the robot's speed, and who carries the delay if it breaks down?

What's still hype: self-replicating data centers, a humanoid on your deck, and any claim that labor is the main bottleneck. Altman's own host pushed on permits and parts. Power and equipment lead times, covered in our look at [how power delays hit electrical and sitework subs](/posts/2026-10-29-anthropic-s1-power-delays-grid-upgrade-costs-construction-electrical-sitework-subs-utility-delay-clauses), will still set many schedules.

**Takeaway:** Pick one repetitive drilling or layout scope on your next hyperscale bid and ask the GC how a robot changes it. The answer tells you where your margin or risk moves.
