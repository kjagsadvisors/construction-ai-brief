Bedrock Robotics says its retrofitted excavators are now doing paid earthwork on active infrastructure jobs in Texas and Nevada with no operator in the cab — not a supervised pilot, but work a general contractor is billing a client for. For a sitework or excavation sub, that changes the retrofit-vs-hire question from a hypothetical to a fleet decision worth pricing out this year.

## What is Bedrock actually selling?

Bedrock's "Operator" system is an aftermarket retrofit, not a new machine. It's a roof-mounted sensor bar — 360-degree LiDAR, RTK-GPS antennas, an inertial measurement unit, and eight HD cameras — paired with an in-cab computer, installed on an excavator a contractor already owns in a single work shift with no permanent modification. It works on 20- to 80-ton class excavators, which covers most mass-excavation and utility-trenching fleets. The company is deployed on three named projects: a water treatment facility in Nevada with Sundt Construction, a multi-million-cubic-yard earthwork site in Texas with Champion Site Prep, and a 1.2-million-cubic-yard civil sitework job with Zachry Construction Corporation. Separately, Bedrock and Sundt are running what the company calls the industry's largest known supervised mass-excavation deployment on a 130-acre Phoenix manufacturing site, with more than 65,000 cubic yards moved so far.

## How autonomous is "no operator in the cab," really?

Not unsupervised in the sense of no human anywhere near the loop. A proximity system stops the machine automatically if a person or object gets within a set distance, and it stays stopped until the area is clear. Separately, a person monitoring the fleet remotely can step in if a machine gets stuck or hits something the system hasn't seen before; Bedrock describes that intervention as relatively rare, after roughly a year of supervised testing before these jobs went live. The honest read: the seat is empty, but the job still has a person watching — just not one riding in the machine.

## What does this change for a sitework sub's crew and schedule?

Sundt CEO Cade Rowley put the value case in scheduling terms, not headcount terms: the partnership is "helping us tackle some of the challenges that matter most in construction: making schedules and costs more predictable, getting more consistent use out of our equipment, and allowing our skilled crews to focus on the work where their experience matters most." That's the realistic near-term pitch — not zero operators, but a fleet that can run more hours per machine (no shift changes, no fatigue-driven slowdowns on repetitive mass-excavation cuts) while your experienced operators move to grading, utility work, and anything that needs judgment calls the system can't make yet.

Before evaluating a retrofit, a sitework sub should be able to answer:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What's my mix of repetitive mass-excavation cuts vs. tight, judgment-heavy digging (utility crossings, shoring, confined access)? | Retrofit economics work best on the former; the latter still needs an operator today |
| What's my current cost per cubic yard moved, fully loaded with labor? | You need this baseline before a subscription-plus-hardware quote means anything |
| Does my GL and workers' comp carrier have any position on unmanned heavy equipment? | Ask before you deploy, not after an incident |
| Who's the remote supervisor if I run this — Bedrock's team or mine? | Determines who's accountable for a delayed intervention |
| What does the equipment do in a power/connectivity loss on a rural site? | Fleet software depends on connectivity your current machines don't need |

## What's unsettled on liability and insurance?

OSHA hasn't written a standard specific to autonomous earthmoving equipment. Existing equipment rules — 29 CFR 1926.600's inspection and operating requirements — still apply to any excavator on a jobsite, autonomous or not, but they were written with a human operator in mind, and it's not yet clear how "competent person" inspection requirements map onto a machine an operator never sits in. Insurance is similarly early: GL and workers' comp underwriting for heavy equipment has always priced around an operator's training and judgment, and carriers are only starting to figure out how to price a machine whose judgment call is a remote monitor and a proximity sensor. That's not a reason to skip evaluating the technology — it's a reason to loop in your broker and safety officer before the equipment vendor, not after.

Bedrock's deployments are still three named jobs, not an industry standard — a useful contrast to the [humanoid robot funding announcements](/posts/2026-08-25-xpeng-iron-humanoid-robot-funding-construction-robotics-reality-check) that promise construction robots but haven't put a machine on a paid job yet. This one has. The retrofit model — bolt onto equipment you already own, no new machine purchase — is the detail that makes it worth watching now rather than waiting for a fleet-replacement cycle. If you run mass excavation or civil sitework, put "get a cost-per-yard quote against your current crew" on next quarter's ops agenda, and loop in your insurance broker before you loop in the vendor.
