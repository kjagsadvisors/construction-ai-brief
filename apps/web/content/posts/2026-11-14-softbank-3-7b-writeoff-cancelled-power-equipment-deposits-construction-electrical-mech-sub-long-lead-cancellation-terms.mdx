SoftBank wrote off ¥597.2 billion, about $3.7 billion, on November 10 because it had paid in advance for power generation equipment for US AI data centers and then revised the orders. For an electrical or mechanical sub, the lesson is direct: a deposit that holds your place in a manufacturer's queue is usually money you do not get back when the job changes, so the contract has to say who eats it.

## What did SoftBank disclose?

The figures below come from SoftBank's fiscal second-quarter results as reported by [Capacity](https://capacityglobal.com/news/softbank-books-data-centre-loss/) and [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/softbank-loss-7bn-ai-data-centre-power-equipment-writedown).

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Total charge | ¥597.2 billion (about $3.7 billion) |
| Non-refundable advance payments | ¥457 billion |
| Contract changes and settlements | ¥140.2 billion |
| Cumulative advance payments for power equipment by end of September | About $9.5 billion |
| Possible rebates on future purchases | Up to $2.1 billion over ten years |
| Quarterly net result | ¥1.12 trillion loss (about $7.1 billion) |

A SoftBank executive told the earnings call the company "made bridge investments in advance to secure power generation equipment in anticipation of shortage." Then requirements changed. Chief accounting officer Kazuko Kimiwada said the company could not recover the fees when it cancelled orders, per Reuters as cited by The Next Web.

SoftBank did not name the equipment, the suppliers, or the projects. The Wall Street Journal, citing people familiar with the orders, reported more than 13 GW of gas turbines reserved between late 2025 and early 2026, mostly from GE Vernova. SoftBank says it is not shrinking its Ohio campus, and CFO Yoshimitsu Goto said the company is doing "a thorough reassessment" that includes how it buys equipment.

## Why does a sub care about an investor's write-off?

Because the mechanism is the one you use every time you release switchgear, generators, chillers or transformers early. The buyer pays to reserve a production slot. The slot has value to the manufacturer whether or not the buyer shows up. When plans change, the buyer pays for the slot anyway.

SoftBank could absorb that. A sub carrying a six-figure deposit on a generator package for a job that gets redesigned cannot. Two versions of this exposure show up on real projects:

- **You placed the order.** The owner or GC asks for early release to protect schedule, you issue the PO and pay the deposit, and the design changes later. If your subcontract does not make early release a directed, reimbursable action, the cancellation charge sits with you while you argue about it.
- **The owner placed the order.** On owner-furnished equipment, the owner holds the reservation and the cancellation risk. Your risk is different: the gear that arrives may not be the gear in your bid documents, or it may arrive on a new date after a "procurement reassessment" like the one Goto described. We walked through that split on [Anthropic's generator queue purchase](/posts/2026-11-09-anthropic-onepwr-1-7gw-caterpillar-generator-queue-construction-electrical-mech-sub-owner-furnished-equipment-eng-scope).

## Does this free up equipment for everyone else?

Do not count on it. GE Vernova told the Journal the changes had "no negative or positive impact" on the company. The plain reading is that someone else took the slots. We have no sourced evidence that lead times moved, and one cancelled reservation program is not a reason to shorten a lead-time assumption in a bid. We made the scope-change point when [Crusoe cancelled a turbine order](/posts/2026-09-28-crusoe-boom-turbine-cancel-data-center-power-scope-risk) in September.

## What should a sub check on its next long-lead release?

1. **Get the cancellation schedule from the manufacturer in writing.** It is usually a percentage of order value that climbs at each milestone: order acceptance, approved submittals, release to fabrication. Attach it to your quote.
2. **Make early release a written direction.** If you are asked to order before the design is final, get that instruction in writing with an acknowledgment that cancellation and restocking charges are reimbursable.
3. **Check how stored and deposited material gets billed.** Confirm the SOV and the pay application terms let you bill deposits, and what documentation the owner's lender wants.
4. **Read the termination-for-convenience clause for committed costs.** It should cover non-cancellable orders, not only work in place.
5. **On owner-furnished gear, price the swap.** Ask what happens to your scope and schedule if the owner substitutes equipment or moves the delivery date, and put the answer in your qualifications.

**Takeaway:** SoftBank's ¥457 billion in lost advance payments is the largest public example of a rule every purchasing agent already knows. Before your next early release, pull the PO terms and the subcontract side by side and confirm that the party who can change the job is the party who pays when a deposit is forfeited.
