Meta says its emissions rose 45% in 2025 as it built AI data centers. It is now re-evaluating the timeline for its 2030 net-zero goal. One reason it gives: supply chains for low-carbon building materials have been slow to develop. If you pour concrete, set steel, or install MEP equipment on data center jobs, you supply those materials. Before you price the next one, find out whether the carbon requirements in the spec still hold, and what happens if the product they call for isn't available.

## What did Meta's report actually say?

Meta posted its 2026 sustainability report on November 19. The main numbers, as reported by [Trellis](https://trellis.net/article/meta-2026-sustainability-report/), [Axios](https://axios.com/2026/11/19/meta-ai-emissions-rise) and [24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/11/21/ai-may-have-just-broken-metas-climate-promise):

| Measure (2025 vs. 2024) | Change |
|---|---|
| Net emissions | Up 45%, to about 12 million metric tons CO2e |
| Capital goods emissions | Up 58%, tied to data center expansion |
| Purchased goods and services | Up 36% |
| Electricity use | Up about 35%, all matched with clean power |
| Scope 3 vs. 2021 baseline | More than double (target was to stay at or below it) |

Two numbers matter most for contractors. First, Meta matched 100% of its electricity with clean and renewable power, and its emissions still went up 45%. The growth came from what Meta buys and builds, not from the power it runs on. Second, 24/7 Wall St.'s breakdown puts capital goods, which include servers, chips, networking gear, steel and concrete, at 73% of Meta's footprint.

Meta says it is "re-evaluating our timelines, sequencing and interim milestones." Trellis reports that the company's leaders listed three causes: AI growth was faster than expected, clean energy projects stalled in interconnection and permitting queues, and "supply chains for low-carbon infrastructure materials have been slow to develop."

Meta is not alone. Trellis reports that Microsoft's footprint rose 25%, Google's 18% and Amazon's 16%, mostly because of data center expansion. Meta is the first of the four to say it will reconsider its timelines.

## Does this mean data center owners will stop caring about embodied carbon?

Don't assume that. Meta has not dropped the goal. It has said it is reviewing the dates. Its [climate page](https://sustainability.atmeta.com/climate/) still says its latest data center designs carry about 77% less embodied carbon than its original design specs. The page also says Meta supports low-carbon concrete and mass timber and runs a supplier emissions program.

Two outcomes are possible, and you can't tell yet which one your next job will get:

- **Requirements loosen.** If the deadline moves, an owner may drop a hard cap on embodied carbon or let a standard mix substitute when the low-carbon one is short.
- **Requirements tighten on paper.** An owner that is behind on its targets has a reason to measure suppliers more closely. That means more EPD requests and more material documentation at closeout.

We saw the second pattern in July, when [Google's emissions report put construction and supply-chain carbon front and center](/posts/2026-07-04-google-emissions-report-data-center-embodied-carbon-epd-subs) and owners started passing carbon paperwork down to subs.

## What should a concrete, steel or MEP sub do before the next data center bid?

1. **Read the carbon language in the spec.** Look in the concrete, structural steel and general requirements sections for embodied carbon limits, EPD submittals or required low-carbon mix designs. Don't rely on what the last job for the same owner required.
2. **Ask the GC in writing whether those requirements are firm.** If the owner is reviewing its targets, an RFI at bid stage protects you from pricing a premium mix nobody enforces, or from skipping one that is required.
3. **Price the real lead time.** Meta itself says low-carbon materials supply is slow. Confirm with your ready-mix supplier and mill that they can deliver the specified product in the volumes and on the schedule the job needs.
4. **Write a substitution clause into your clarifications.** State what you'll do if the specified low-carbon product isn't available on schedule, who approves the substitute, and who pays the cost difference.
5. **Keep your EPD files current.** However this goes, the documentation request isn't going away. A sub that can hand over EPDs and mill certifications quickly has an easier time getting through prequalification.

## What's still unclear?

When Meta will announce new dates, whether its project specs will change, and whether Microsoft, Google or Amazon will follow. Nothing in the report says how Meta will treat embodied carbon requirements on contracts already signed.

**The takeaway:** the biggest AI builders are falling behind on their carbon goals, and they are partly blaming the material supply chain your shop buys from. Before your next data center bid, confirm in writing what the carbon requirements are, whether the specified materials can actually be delivered, and what happens if they can't.
