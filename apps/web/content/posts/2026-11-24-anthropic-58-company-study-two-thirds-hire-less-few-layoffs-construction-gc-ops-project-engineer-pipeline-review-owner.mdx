Anthropic interviewed people at 58 US companies, construction firms among them, and found that AI almost never cost anyone their job outright. What it changed was hiring: about two-thirds of the companies said they now hire less because of AI. For a GC, the risk is not layoffs. It is quietly skipping the project engineer and project coordinator hires where future PMs learn the work.

## What did Anthropic's report find?

The [report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/working-with-ai), published November 23 by Anthropic's economics team, is based on 66 interviews at 58 companies. All of them are Anthropic enterprise customers that were already adopting AI, so this is not a random sample of American business. The headline numbers:

| Finding | What the report says |
|---|---|
| Companies where AI fully eliminated a job and someone lost it | 2 |
| Companies that pushed out employees who wouldn't adopt AI | 5 |
| Companies that cut contracted or offshore work (developers, customer service, legal, back office) | 9 |
| Companies hiring more because of AI, mostly AI and IT support roles | 10 |
| Companies hiring less because of AI | About two-thirds |
| Share of that hiring decline from not backfilling people who left | About one-third |

The rest of the hiring decline came from companies not adding people they would otherwise have needed as they grew. One interviewee summed it up: "We may not be firing, but we're not hiring."

## Where does construction show up?

The report names construction work a few times without counting how many firms were construction companies. Two examples matter for GC operations:

- **Morning crew check-ins.** At one construction company, project managers used to call crew members at different sites every morning. The company replaced that with "an agentic workflow for checking in across construction sites." The manager told the researchers work "can be a joy again."
- **A contract administrator who automated their own job.** At another construction firm, "a contract administrator helped automate their own data-gathering work and was redeployed." That is the opposite of a layoff, and the person who knew the work built the fix.

Neither example comes with hours saved or error rates, so treat them as signs of what firms are trying rather than results to copy.

## What does this mean for a GC's project engineer pipeline?

Most of the work a project engineer or project coordinator does in their first two years is the kind of work AI tools are now being pointed at: chasing submittals, updating the RFI log, pulling data for pay apps, calling around for status. That grunt work is also how a PE learns what a late submittal does to the schedule and which subs need a second call.

The report shows what goes wrong when the junior seat disappears. One firm dropped junior analyst positions because executives could run their own queries. A law firm took the opposite route: junior lawyers had to take each AI-written first draft and work through it with a senior attorney, because otherwise, as the interviewee asked, "how does an associate know if that's a good draft?"

A GC that skips the next PE hire because the logs now update themselves should answer three questions first:

1. **Who learns the job?** If nobody junior is doing the logs, decide which role will be your PMs in five years and how they get the reps.
2. **Who reviews the agent's output?** The authors say the difficulty of reviewing AI output "came up again and again." On a project, someone has to own checking the submittal log or the check-in summary, by name.
3. **Who builds the automation?** The contract administrator example suggests the person doing the work is the right one to automate it, and is worth keeping afterward.

## What are the limits of this report?

It is qualitative, the companies chose to buy AI from the company that wrote the report, and the value estimates are self-reported, not measured. The authors say none of it rules out future job losses and that follow-up quantitative work is needed. It says nothing about field trades, and nothing here supports cutting a crew.

We looked at the shop-floor side of the same question when [P&G expanded factory shifts with nobody on the floor](/posts/2026-11-24-procter-gamble-unattended-factory-shifts-15-sites-construction-trade-sub-prefab-shop-overnight-one-machine-test). In both cases the people move to setup and review. They don't go away.

**Takeaway:** Before you cancel or delay a project engineer or coordinator hire because an AI tool now handles the logs, write down who will review that tool's output on each job and how your next PMs will learn the work. If you can't answer both, make the hire.
