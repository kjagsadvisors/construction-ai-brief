An apartment complex's AI assistant told a tenant that paying her overdue rent on November 30 "works" — but a court had already given the landlord possession of her unit weeks earlier, and she was locked out anyway. For a trade sub letting AI answer office email, that's the risk to plan for: the assistant writes a confident yes based only on what it can see. Anything about payment dates, schedule, pricing or scope should go out under a person's name, after a person reads it.

## What happened in Raleigh?

According to [Moneywise reporting republished by Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/real-estate/articles/raleigh-woman-just-got-evicted-220000991.html), which cites WRAL News, Colony Townhome Apartments emailed tenant Laura Mack on November 8 asking, "What date works for you to pay the full $3,934.17?" She asked to pay at the end of the month. The reply: "Paying on November 30, the last day of the month, works."

She got an eviction notice on November 16. When she called the leasing office, an agent told her the emails had come from Olivia, the AI assistant the complex had introduced to residents in late 2025. [Atlanta Black Star](https://atlantablackstar.com/2026/11/25/north-carolina-woman-evicted-after-ai-mishap/) reports she contested it on November 19 and lost the apartment.

Three details matter for anyone running AI on a company inbox:

1. **The assistant didn't know the account's real status.** A company spokesperson said a Wake County court had already granted possession on October 23, and that "a payment arrangement would not change the court-ordered lockout." The AI agreed to a date for a debt that was already in a court process.
2. **The emails weren't labeled.** Olivia's messages are normally marked as AI-written, but these weren't. One mentioned a leak in her bathroom and expressed sympathy, so she assumed a person wrote it.
3. **Nobody has said what Olivia was allowed to agree to.** The spokesperson neither confirmed nor denied that an AI wrote the emails, and didn't describe any policy on its messages.

## Where does this show up in a trade sub's office?

More office managers are turning on AI features that draft or send email replies, and a sub's inbox is full of questions where a short "yes, that works" has money behind it:

| Incoming email | A confident AI reply | What it may not know |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier: "Can we expect payment Friday?" | "Friday works." | You're waiting on the GC's pay app, or the invoice is disputed |
| GC: "Can you mobilize Monday?" | "Yes, we'll see you Monday." | The crew is committed elsewhere; the submittal isn't approved |
| Customer: "Is the price still good?" | "Your quote stands." | The quote expired, or copper went up |
| GC: "Can you absorb this as part of base scope?" | "Understood, we'll take care of it." | It's a change, and you just waived the change order |
| Supplier: "Can we substitute this valve?" | "That should be fine." | The spec or the engineer has to approve it |

As in Raleigh, the AI sees the email thread, not the account hold, the schedule board or the contract's notice clause.

## What should the office set up before turning AI loose on email?

- **Draft, don't send, on anything with money, dates or scope.** Most email AI tools can be set to suggest a reply rather than send it. Use that for every external message that answers "when," "how much" or "is this included."
- **Label AI-written messages.** If the tool sends anything on its own, mark it. The missing label is what made Mack think a person had agreed.
- **Give it a short "never agree to" list in writing.** Payment dates, schedule commitments, price confirmations, scope additions, substitutions, and anything touching a disputed account or a lien.
- **Check the reply against your systems, not the thread.** A payment-date reply gets checked against AP aging; a mobilization yes against the schedule.

## What are the limits here?

This is one case, reported mostly from the tenant's side; she acknowledges she owed the rent and is still fighting in court, and the landlord says the court order, not the email, decided the outcome. It's also a residential lease, not a construction contract. Whether an AI-written email binds a company depends on the contract and the facts, and that's a question for your attorney, not us. North Carolina State Sen. Jim Burgin, who has pushed a bill requiring businesses to disclose AI interactions, [put it this way](https://atlantablackstar.com/2026/11/25/north-carolina-woman-evicted-after-ai-mishap/): "notification is one thing, and I think liability is another."
