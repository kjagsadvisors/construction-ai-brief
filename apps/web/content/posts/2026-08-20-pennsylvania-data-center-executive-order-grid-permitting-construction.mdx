Pennsylvania will no longer review a data center permit application until the developer has signed a binding local-hiring and community-benefits commitment and secured local approval first. For a GC or trade contractor pricing data center work in the state, that paperwork now has to exist before there's a permit to build a schedule around.

## What did Shapiro's executive order actually change?

Governor Josh Shapiro signed Executive Order 2026-05 on August 18, covering any data center with peak electricity demand above 25 megawatts — the size of nearly every hyperscale campus currently proposed in the state. Two changes matter for a construction schedule:

- **Fast-track permitting is gone.** The order immediately pulls every AI data center project out of Pennsylvania's Permit Fast Track Program and bars new ones from entering it.
- **DEP won't open the file without a signed commitment.** The Department of Environmental Protection is directed to evaluate a permit application only after the developer has made a legally binding commitment to the state's GRID Requirements — the Governor's Responsible Infrastructure Development standards — and won local approval.

Shapiro's office has said more than 100 data center proposals are currently circulating in Pennsylvania, and that most are speculative — no signed power agreement, no financing, no site control. This order is a filter: developers who won't commit to GRID don't get a permit reviewed at all.

## What do the GRID Requirements actually require?

Five categories, and two of them land directly on a GC's scope and schedule:

| GRID category | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Workforce & economic development | Hire and train local workers; sign a community benefit agreement covering schools, infrastructure, and long-term local investment |
| Transparency | No NDAs on data center projects; annual reporting of energy and water use to DEP |
| Energy affordability | Preference for projects that bring new power supply, including firm clean generation, rather than drawing on existing grid capacity |
| Environmental protection | Standard DEP review criteria, now gated behind the GRID commitment |
| Community engagement | Local approval required before DEP will evaluate the permit |

The workforce line is the one a GC needs written into the contract, not assumed. "Hire and train local workers" is a developer commitment to the state — but the GC is who actually staffs the job. If a developer signs a GRID commitment promising local hiring targets it can't back up with a contractor who can deliver them, that gap becomes the GC's problem during a compliance audit, not the developer's.

## What does this mean for the tax exemption a lot of these deals are priced around?

Pennsylvania's Department of Revenue is updating the Computer Data Center Equipment Exemption Program — the sales-and-use tax exemption on data center equipment — to require GRID compliance as a condition of eligibility. A developer that skips the commitment doesn't just lose the fast permit path; it loses the tax exemption the project's financial model was likely built on. That's a pro forma problem before it's a construction problem, and it's worth a GC or precon lead confirming the exemption assumption is still valid before finalizing a bid.

## Should a GC change anything on a bid this week?

Not the price, yet — nothing in the order retroactively touches projects with permits already issued. But before pricing new data center work in Pennsylvania, confirm two things with the developer: whether the GRID commitment and local approval are signed or still being negotiated, and whether the project's tax exemption assumption accounts for the new compliance condition. Those two items now decide when DEP opens the file, which is the date everything else on the schedule gets built backward from. The same pattern is playing out in [Fort Worth and Louisville](/posts/2026-08-17-data-center-moratorium-fort-worth-louisville-backlash-trust), where local backlash forced outright moratoriums — Pennsylvania's version is a state government converting that same backlash into a permitting checklist instead of a stop-work order.
