OpenAI is designing and building its own data center for the first time, instead of leasing finished capacity the way it does everywhere else — and the executive running the project says the resulting design will be published for anyone in the industry to use. For the general contractors and construction managers who build hyperscale data centers, that's two changes in one announcement: a new client sourcing its own build team directly, and a design playbook that might stop being anyone's proprietary edge.

## What did OpenAI actually announce?

On July 22, OpenAI unveiled Project Camellia, a data center campus on roughly 1,400 acres in Effingham County, Georgia, inside the Savannah Gateway Industrial Hub. The company has contracted with Georgia Power for up to 3.2 gigawatts of electricity, delivered in phases from 2028 through 2032. OpenAI's VP of compute strategy, Sachin Katti, put the fully built cost above $30 billion. OpenAI is also committing $80 million to local schools, public safety, health care, workforce training, and housing, plus up to $71 million in AI credits for students at Georgia colleges near the site.

None of that alone would be unusual — hyperscale data center campuses of this size have become routine news this year, from [Meta's Alberta campus](https://constructionaibrief.com/posts/2026-07-14-meta-alberta-data-center-gas-plant-dry-cooling-construction) to the [half-built, permit-stalled Project Jupiter in New Mexico](https://constructionaibrief.com/posts/2026-07-19-project-jupiter-pipeline-rejected-data-center-critical-path). What's different here is who's holding the pen.

## Why does it matter that OpenAI is building this one itself?

Everywhere else, OpenAI buys compute — it leases capacity inside data centers that Oracle, Amazon Web Services, CoreWeave, and other operators design, finance, and build. Project Camellia flips that: OpenAI is the principal designer and builder, and it's now searching for its own financing and construction partners to scale the project to full size. That means the GC or CM contract for this campus, and presumably for whatever OpenAI builds next under this model, gets sourced directly by OpenAI rather than by a data center developer acting as the middleman. If you're a firm with a hyperscale data center group — Turner, DPR, Holder, Mortenson, Clayco, JE Dunn, and the rest of that short list — the buyer you'd be pitching just changed, and the RFP may not run through the channels you're used to.

## What does "public blueprints" mean for the GCs who build these things?

This is the part worth watching closest. Katti has said publicly that OpenAI intends to develop uniform data center blueprints and publish them for the broader industry, on the theory that shared design lessons cut both cost and build time. A hyperscale data center's design — power density, cooling topology, structural loading, electrical distribution — has historically been a proprietary asset that a handful of design-build GCs iterate on job after job, and it's a real part of why those firms win repeat work at premium fees. If OpenAI publishes a reference design that performs well, that specific edge gets easier to replicate.

| | Leased-capacity model (Oracle, AWS, most hyperscalers) | OpenAI's self-build model (Project Camellia) |
|---|---|---|
| Who hires the GC | Data center developer/operator | OpenAI directly |
| Who owns the design | Developer, often proprietary | OpenAI, explicitly to be published |
| Where the RFP surfaces | Developer's existing GC relationships | OpenAI's own construction-partner search |
| Design as competitive edge | Yes — repeat-client design IP | Diminished if the blueprint is public |

## Should a mid-size GC try to get in on this?

Realistically, Project Camellia's scale — $30 billion, four buildings, 3.2 GW — puts the general contract with the handful of firms that already run national hyperscale data center groups. But the labor and subcontractor side is more open: OpenAI has explicitly committed to prioritizing local contractors and suppliers during both construction and operations, and Effingham County will be competing for craft labor against other Georgia mega-projects already drawing from the same regional pool. A regional mechanical, electrical, or sitework sub within reach of Rincon has a real reason to get in front of whichever GC wins the work now, before subcontractor packages get let. Estimators tracking data center opportunities should also flag that the full $30 billion-plus build is still contingent on OpenAI landing its construction partners — the project isn't fully staffed yet, which means the RFP window on parts of it may still be open.
