On June 12 at 5:21 pm Eastern, Anthropic received a US government export control directive with a blunt requirement: disable access to Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 — immediately, for every user on earth.

Fable 5 had launched three days earlier as Anthropic's most capable public model. It was built on Mythos 5, a "Mythos-class" system with capabilities in cybersecurity research serious enough to trigger national security export controls. The government's stated concern: a specific jailbreak technique had been identified that could unlock those cybersecurity capabilities. Foreign nationals having access to the model was deemed a risk.

To comply, Anthropic couldn't simply block foreign users — it disabled both models for all customers worldwide. Paying subscribers, API users, and every product built on top of Anthropic's models went dark the same evening. According to Anthropic's own statement: "The net effect of this order is that we must abruptly disable Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all our customers to ensure compliance."

Eight days later, as of June 20, the models are still offline. Anthropic is working toward a deal with Trump administration officials but has given no confirmed restoration date.

## Why this matters beyond the tech press

Most contractors won't use Claude Fable 5 directly. But this event exposes something that applies to every AI tool in a construction operation: you almost certainly don't know which AI model powers the tools your team uses every day.

Contract review tools. RFI drafting assistants. Submittal log automation. Daily log generators. Each of these calls a third-party foundation model API on the backend — the AI layer is invisible to the end user but essential to the workflow. When the underlying model becomes unavailable — whether by government order, financial failure, pricing change, or a technical crisis — the workflow stops.

Anthropic's outage is the first large-scale, involuntary test of this dependency at real scale. It's unlikely to be the last. AI models are now subject to the same export control regime as advanced semiconductors and encryption software. A directive can arrive at any time.

## The three questions to ask every AI tool vendor

Before you build a critical-path workflow around any AI tool, these questions belong in your evaluation:

1. **Which model does your tool run on?** If the vendor won't answer this, treat it as a red flag. You're not asking for proprietary details — you're asking to understand the dependency chain.

2. **What happens to my workflows if that model becomes unavailable?** A specific answer names a fallback: a secondary provider, a local model, or a degradation mode that at least surfaces a clear error. Vague answers ("we'd switch providers quickly") mean no tested plan exists.

3. **Have you tested a failover?** Knowing a backup exists and having tested it under realistic conditions are different things. Vendors who have drilled it will be specific about what degrades and what doesn't.

The best-positioned tools are multi-model: they route requests across multiple providers — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, open-weight models — and fail over automatically. You don't get identical performance from every model, but the workflow keeps running. This architecture is more common in tools marketed to regulated industries. It's starting to make sense for construction AI as well.

## Auditing what you already have

Pull up the list of AI tools your team is actively using in production workflows — including tools that started as experiments and crept into real work. For each one, run through the three questions above.

The Trimble/Document Crunch acquisition is a useful frame for why this matters: when a major platform buys an AI tool, the vendor relationships and model dependencies get inherited along with the product. [We broke down what Trimble actually bought and what it means for mid-market contractors](/posts/2026-05-14-tool-teardown-document-crunch) earlier this year — that analysis applies here too.

You don't need to rebuild anything now. But if a vendor tells you they run on a single model with no fallback, document the backup procedure for that workflow. "We revert to the previous process" is a valid plan — provided someone has written down what the previous process was.

AI is working its way into enough construction operations that this kind of vendor audit is starting to look like standard procurement due diligence. The Anthropic shutdown made the case in real time.
