Fort Worth and Louisville both moved to block new data center construction the same week Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei told an investor, in public, that the AI industry's backlash problem is "fundamentally a crisis of trust." For a GC with data center work in the backlog or the pipeline, that's not a coincidence worth a shrug — it's confirmation that the resistance showing up at your entitlement hearings isn't a messaging problem the industry will PR its way out of.

## What happened this week

Fort Worth's city council voted unanimously on August 11 to begin a 90-day moratorium on new data center construction, set to take effect early next year and extendable to 180 days, so the city can write development rules covering water use, noise, and land impact. Louisville Metro Council went further two days later, voting 24-1 on August 13 to freeze approval of any new data center proposal for six months or until it passes comprehensive zoning rules — whichever comes first.

| Jurisdiction | Action | Vote date | Duration | Existing projects |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fort Worth, TX | 90-day moratorium on new proposals | Aug 11, 2026 | 90 days, extendable to 180 | Exempted — pipeline continues |
| Louisville Metro, KY | Freeze on new proposal approvals | Aug 13, 2026 | 6 months or until new zoning passes | New applications only |
| National (Q1 2026) | 75 projects blocked or delayed, ~$130B value | Jan–Mar 2026 | — | — |

Neither city is stopping work already underway. Fort Worth has four data centers operating and one under construction inside city limits, and all of that keeps moving. What both councils froze is the next proposal — the one still in precon, still being priced, still looking for a GC.

## The backlash isn't local anymore

Fort Worth and Louisville aren't outliers. Data Center Watch, which tracks opposition nationally, counted at least 75 data center projects worth roughly $130 billion blocked or delayed in the first quarter of 2026 alone — matching the total for all of 2025 in a single quarter. The number of organized opposition groups more than doubled, from 396 at the end of 2025 to 833 across 49 states by March. State legislatures filed more than 300 data center bills in the first six weeks of the year, with statewide moratorium proposals introduced in 14 states.

## What Amodei's comment adds

On August 16, responding to investor Gavin Baker's argument that AI leaders' own risk warnings have fueled public skepticism of data centers, Amodei pushed back with a specific diagnosis: "I think it is fundamentally a crisis of trust. I think that ordinary people don't trust companies, governments, or the tech industry and always suspect that we are cooking up some new way to screw them over."

That matters for a GC's planning, not because Amodei runs a construction company, but because he's naming the failure mode correctly: this isn't a communications problem AI companies can fix with a better ad campaign, and it isn't isolated to a handful of loud counties. If the industry's own CEOs are describing it as structural distrust rather than a messaging gap, a GC should plan for it to keep showing up at planning commissions, not fade after a few cycles of local news.

## What to actually do about it

Nothing about this changes what's already under contract. What it should change is bid/no-bid diligence on new data center pursuits:

1. **Check moratorium status before pricing precon work.** A jurisdiction with an active or pending moratorium proposal is a schedule risk before it's a construction risk — Data Center Watch's tracker and local council agendas are both public.
2. **Weight backlog concentration.** If a meaningful share of projected data center revenue sits in not-yet-entitled projects in a single contested market, that's concentration risk worth flagging to leadership, the same way a GC would flag single-owner or single-market exposure.
3. **Ask the developer about community engagement, not just permits.** Projects that survive local review increasingly do so because the developer ran a real public process — water use disclosures, tax terms, jobs commitments — before the vote, not after opposition organized.

The FERC show-cause order [we covered Monday](/posts/2026-08-17-ferc-large-load-show-cause-order-data-center-interconnection-deadline) is a federal-level fix aimed at grid interconnection rules. Fort Worth and Louisville are the other half of the risk: local political risk that federal rule changes don't touch at all.
