METR, the AI evaluation nonprofit known for its chart of how long a task AI agents can finish, published a major update on November 20. The short version for a precon team: AI agents now usually match or beat skilled people on work with a clear right answer, but still show worse judgment than experts when there's no clear way to check the result. That splits a bid into two piles. Give AI the parts you can check. Keep the scope, risk and go/no-go calls with your estimators.

## What did METR actually measure?

METR times how long a task takes a skilled human, then measures whether AI agents can finish it. Per the [Q4 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-20-q4-2026-capability-update/):

- **Longer tasks.** On its combined task suite, METR estimates a 50% time horizon of about 40 hours for GPT-6 Astra and 39 hours for Opus 5.5. That means roughly a coin-flip chance on tasks that take a skilled person a full work week. The 95% intervals run from about 12 hours to well over 1,000, so treat the point estimate loosely.
- **Faster growth.** Time horizons are "doubling roughly every 3.61 months."
- **Checkable work.** "Models usually match or outperform skilled humans on our coding and optimization tasks," where success can be scored.
- **Judgment work.** On tasks without clear feedback, "AI agents appear to have worse judgment than human experts."
- **AI graders.** "AI judges are overgenerous when judging tasks made by models stronger than themselves." In one test, Opus 5.5 scored GPT-6 Astra's work about 0.2 points out of 1 higher than the human rater did.

[MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/11/20/1149399/the-most-misunderstood-graph-in-ai-just-got-a-major-update/) adds that when models were asked to design new AI evaluations, none of the outputs were good enough for METR staff to use.

## What does this mean for estimators?

None of METR's tasks involve drawings or specs. The pattern still maps onto a bid. The question to ask of each step is: can someone check this answer against something known?

| Bid step | Checkable? | Where AI fits |
|---|---|---|
| Counting fixtures, devices, doors from a schedule | Yes, against the drawings | Draft, then spot-check |
| Linear footage and area takeoff | Yes, against a manual sample | Draft, then spot-check |
| Extending unit prices and totals | Yes, the math reconciles | Good fit |
| Pulling spec requirements with section numbers | Yes, if every line cites a page | Good fit with citations |
| Spotting scope gaps between trades | Partly | Flag list for a person to review |
| Writing exclusions and qualifications | No clear right answer | Human decides |
| Pricing risk, contingency, go/no-go | No clear right answer | Human decides |

The top half is where an AI tool is most likely to earn its keep. The bottom half is where METR's finding says to be careful. A model that confidently writes a clean exclusion list may still miss the one that matters on this job, and nothing will tell you it did.

## Can a second AI check the first one's work?

This is the most practical finding in the update. It is tempting to run one model's takeoff or spec summary past a second model as a reviewer. METR found AI judges grade stronger models' work too generously. If your "check" is another AI saying it looks right, you are not getting much of a check.

Better checks use a known answer:

1. **Re-run a closed bid.** Feed the tool the drawings from a job you already estimated and compare its quantities to your final counts.
2. **Hand-count a sample.** Pick two sheets and count them yourself before trusting the rest.
3. **Require citations.** Every spec item should carry a section and page number so a person can verify it in seconds.

We made a similar point when we covered [AI models dropping correct answers under pushback](/posts/2026-11-18-unesco-ucl-ai-models-concede-first-pushback-construction-estimator-spec-scope-questions-fresh-chat-quote-the-section): the fix is to anchor the model to the document, not to its own confidence.

## What are the limits of this data?

- **Not construction tasks.** METR tests software, optimization, games and research tasks.
- **Estimated hours.** Only 9% of the human task times come directly from people doing the work. The authors call some estimates "quite speculative."
- **Wide error bars.** The 40-hour figure could be far lower or far higher.
- **Judgment is hard to score.** METR's own staff told MIT Technology Review that judgment-heavy tasks are much harder to quantify, so that gap may be measured less precisely than the coding gains.

**Takeaway:** On your next bid, sort each step into "checkable" or "judgment" before you hand anything to an AI tool. Let it draft the checkable steps, verify them against the drawings or a closed bid rather than a second AI, and keep exclusions, contingency and the go/no-go call with your senior estimator.
