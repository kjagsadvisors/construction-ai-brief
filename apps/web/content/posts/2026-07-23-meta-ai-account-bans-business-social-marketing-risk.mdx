Meta's automated content moderation has been wrongly suspending and deleting real Facebook and Instagram accounts — including small business pages — and routing the appeals through the same AI systems that issued the ban in the first place. That's not an abstract AI-safety story if your business's entire project portfolio and lead pipeline lives on one of those pages, which is exactly how a lot of remodelers, specialty subs, and small GCs run their marketing today.

## What did Meta actually get wrong?

Facebook and Instagram users have been telling the BBC for weeks that their accounts were disabled with no clear reason and no way to reach a person. Meta acknowledged a "technical error" that wrongly suspended some Facebook Groups, but people contacting reporters say the problem runs wider than that admission covers — hitting individual profiles and business pages, not just Groups. More than 25,000 people have signed an online petition, started by a Canadian user whose own Facebook account was disabled for nine days in May before being reinstated, asking Meta to restore human review of ban decisions.

Meta has leaned harder on AI moderation over the past year, cutting the number of human reviewers as automated systems took on a larger share of enforcement decisions across Facebook, Instagram, and Threads. Meta's own position, stated in a company blog post, is that its newest models make 13% fewer enforcement mistakes and catch 10% more actual violations than human reviewers did, based on internal tests run since March 2026.

## Why can't a banned business just get someone on the phone?

Because the appeal usually goes to the same kind of system that issued the ban. Meta's own Oversight Board — the semi-independent body Meta set up to review its content decisions — said in June 2026 that the account-ban process lacks due process and transparency. In several of the cases reporters have documented, accounts were only restored after a journalist contacted Meta directly to ask about that specific case — not through the normal appeals flow available to everyone else.

Meta says the accounts caught up in the recent wave of complaints were flagged by its older moderation systems, not the newer, more accurate ones it's rolling out. That may be true, and it's a real distinction. It doesn't change what a business owner is looking at today: an account disabled with a form-letter denial, no reviewer to call, and no guarantee that pressing "appeal" does anything but ask the same system to check its own work again.

## Where does this actually touch a construction business?

Directly, for the segment of the industry that has shifted its marketing onto social platforms over the past several years — which is most residential remodelers, many specialty subs building a hiring pipeline, and small GCs courting homeowner work.

| What's built on the platform | What a wrongful ban takes with it |
|---|---|
| Instagram/Facebook page as project portfolio | Years of before/after photos, the visual case for your work to a new lead |
| Follower base built through referrals and tagged jobs | The audience you spent years growing, gone with the account |
| Facebook trade groups used for hiring or subcontractor leads | Access to the network you use to staff jobs |
| DMs and comments as the lead-intake channel | Every unanswered inquiry sitting in a suspended inbox |

None of that is exotic — it's the normal working setup for a business that markets itself on social media instead of (or in addition to) a website. A wrongful ban doesn't just cost a day of downtime; it can erase the portfolio and the audience that portfolio took years to build, with an appeals process that may not involve a human at any point.

## What should a contractor running social media do about it?

1. **Back up your portfolio somewhere you control.** Keep original project photos and client testimonials on your own drive or website — not just posted to Instagram — so a platform-side wipeout doesn't take your entire case history with it.
2. **Verify through Meta Business Suite.** A verified business account gives Meta more signal that the page belongs to a real, operating business, which can matter if you do have to appeal.
3. **Don't make the account your only lead-intake channel.** Route inquiries to an email or phone number you control, even if Instagram is where the lead first sees your work, so a suspended account doesn't also cut off leads already in your pipeline.
4. **Document your account before there's a problem.** Screenshots or exports of your page, follower count, and post history give you something concrete to point to if you ever do reach a human reviewer.
