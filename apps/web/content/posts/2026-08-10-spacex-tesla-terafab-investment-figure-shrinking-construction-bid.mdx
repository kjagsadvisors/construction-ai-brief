SpaceX and Tesla confirmed on August 6 that their jointly backed chip fab — Terafab — will rise in Grimes County, Texas, at a first-phase price tag of $16.8 billion. That number is already smaller than the one SpaceX put in writing to its own IPO investors ten weeks earlier. If you're a GC or trade sub sizing up whether to chase this work, the number worth pricing off isn't in either press release — it's in the county tax agreement, and it's smaller still.

## What is Terafab, and who's actually building it?

Terafab is a planned 100-million-square-foot semiconductor campus roughly an hour outside Houston — a vertically integrated site meant to handle chip logic, memory, advanced packaging, and testing under one roof, built out over four phases. Despite the "SpaceX/Tesla chip factory" framing in most coverage, Intel is the builder and operator: Intel Foundry signed Tesla, SpaceX, and xAI on in April 2026 as anchor customers on its 18A process node, in what's widely described as the most significant external foundry contract in Intel's history. Tesla wants the chips for Optimus and Cybercab; SpaceX and xAI want them for satellite and space-based compute.

## Why does the investment figure keep shrinking?

| Date | Source | Figure cited |
|---|---|---|
| May 2026 | SpaceX IPO (S-1) filing | $55 billion initial investment; up to $119 billion with all phases |
| June 2026 | Grimes County PILOT agreement | $5 billion minimum by 2030, 1,800 jobs by 2035 (the only enforceable figures) |
| August 6, 2026 | Company/Governor Abbott announcement | $16.8 billion "first phase," 3,000 jobs |

SpaceX's own S-1 told IPO investors it would invest $55 billion to get Terafab's first phase running — and added the caveat that "there is no assurance it will meet its Terafab objectives within expected timelines, or at all." Two months later, the confirmed first-phase number is $16.8 billion, about a third of that. None of that is illegal or even unusual for a megaproject at this stage — investment figures for projects this size get revised constantly as design and financing firm up. But it means the press-release number and the SEC-filing number have already disagreed once by nearly $40 billion, and the number Grimes County can actually enforce — $5 billion by 2030 — is smaller than both.

## What's actually locked in, on paper

The Grimes County agreement, approved in June 2026, trades a 100% property tax abatement on buildings and equipment (2027–2036) for two hard commitments: SpaceX invests at least $5 billion by 2030, and creates at least 1,800 jobs by 2035. SpaceX also owes the county $10 million up front and roughly $20 million a year for 35 years in lieu of property taxes. Separately, the state extended a $30 million Texas Enterprise Fund grant. Those are the numbers a bank, a bonding company, or a GC's risk committee should treat as real. The $16.8 billion and 3,000-job figures are the company's stated ambition for phase one, not a contractual floor.

## What a chip fab build asks of a GC or trade sub that a data center doesn't

Semiconductor fab work isn't warehouse-and-racks construction. It requires:

- **Ultrapure water systems and reuse infrastructure** — Terafab is sourcing from the Gibbons Creek Reservoir rather than local groundwater, with on-site wastewater treatment and reuse built into the design, not bolted on later.
- **Cleanroom-grade mechanical and electrical work**, built to tolerances well beyond typical industrial or even data center specs.
- **Specialty gas delivery piping** for lithography and etch tools.
- **Vibration-controlled foundations**, since lithography tools are sensitive to movement measured in nanometers.
- **Millwrights specifically** — county officials have already flagged this trade as the tightest bottleneck for the build-out, on top of the general skilled-trade competition every hyperscale project in Texas is currently running against.

## Should a mid-size GC or sub go after this work?

If you have semiconductor, cleanroom, or advanced-MEP experience, yes — pursue it, but scope your commitment (subcontracts, workforce hiring, equipment purchases) against the $5 billion/1,800-job floor in the county agreement, not the $16.8 billion headline. If phase one slips or shrinks the way the investment figure already has once, the enforceable contract is what survives; the press-release number is not. For everyone else, the more durable takeaway is the trade squeeze: another multi-billion-dollar Texas megaproject just entered the market chasing the same millwrights, pipefitters, and electricians that data center and power-plant work in the state is already competing for. Price your own labor availability accordingly, even if you never touch this job.
