A lawsuit filed this week says an AI alert was cancelled and armed police showed up anyway. Jobsites with AI-monitored camera towers often run the same chain: software flags a person, someone checks the video, someone calls the police. Before the next 5 a.m. concrete pour, the project team should know how that chain gets called off.

## What happened in the Omnilert case?

Four students sued Omnilert, the maker of an AI gun-detection system, along with their school district, the county police department and individual officers, according to the [Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law](https://www.lawyerscommittee.org/lawyers-committee-files-lawsuit-on-behalf-of-black-students-detained-and-searched-due-to-ai-alert-falsely-identifying-a-bag-of-chips-as-a-firearm/), which represents them. The incident dates to October 2025. The system flagged a student's bag of chips as a possible firearm and reported a 90% probability of a gun on campus.

The complaint says staff and police looked at the image, saw the mistake, and cancelled the alert. Armed officers still came. The students were ordered to their knees, handcuffed and searched. The suit alleges there was no adequate protocol to keep a cancelled alert from triggering an armed response, and that officials knew the system often produced false alerts, per [theGrio's report](https://thegrio.com/2026/11/17/baltimore-teens-sue-omnilert-school-district-false-alert/). These are allegations from the plaintiffs' side. Nothing has been decided.

The detail that matters for contractors isn't the AI's miss. Every detection system has misses. The problem was that a person caught the error and the cancellation didn't reach the people already on their way.

## How is a jobsite camera tower set up the same way?

Remote-monitored towers on commercial jobs usually run a similar sequence. In the setup [Noonlight describes for TrueLook](https://www.noonlight.com/customer-stories/truelook-jobsite-security), cameras detect motion in chosen zones after hours. A trained agent reviews the event. Workers clocking in, deliveries and harmless loitering get dismissed. Verified threats get called in to police with live video and site information. In one example the vendor cites, police were notified 1 minute 13 seconds after motion was detected.

That speed is the selling point, and the vendor's process puts a person between the AI flag and the 911 call, which is the right design. But it also means police can arrive on site before your super knows anything happened. Who gets flagged is often your own people: the electrician's foreman picking up a gang box at 4:30 a.m., a sub's crew starting early to beat the heat, a pump truck driver arriving ahead of the pour.

## What should the project team ask the monitoring provider?

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How is a dispatch cancelled after police have been called? | This is the step the lawsuit says broke down. |
| Who on our side can cancel, and by what channel? | A phone tree that rings the PM at 2 a.m. is not a plan. |
| What after-hours schedule do you have for this site? | Early starts, weekend work and night pours should be in the agent's hands, not only in the three-week lookahead. |
| What does the agent see before calling police? | Ask whether they can tell a hard hat and vest from an intruder, and whether they call the site contact first. |
| Who pays false-alarm fines, and who carries liability for a bad dispatch? | Check the rental or service agreement, not the brochure. |

## What should a GC do this week?

1. **Send the monitoring company a current after-hours list** for every active site: crews, delivery windows, special pours, and a live contact for each night.
2. **Make it part of the weekly sub meeting.** Any sub planning early or weekend work tells the GC by a set day so the list gets updated.
3. **Get the cancel procedure in writing** and post it in the site trailer next to the emergency contacts.
4. **Tell crews what to do if police arrive.** Stop, keep hands visible, and point to the super's number. Nobody argues it out at the gate.

We wrote earlier about how [AI safety tools are spreading faster than proof they work](/posts/2026-11-01-ai-jobsite-safety-evidence-thin-fyld-amey-construction-gc-pilot-metrics). Security monitoring has the same gap: vendors publish response times, not how often they called police on the wrong person. The lesson from the Omnilert case is that the human check only protects anyone if the "never mind" moves as fast as the alarm. Ask your provider how fast theirs is.
