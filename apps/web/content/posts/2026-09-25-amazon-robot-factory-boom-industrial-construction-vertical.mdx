Amazon just announced its fourth robot factory in the US in three years — a $100 million-plus retrofit in Greenwood, Indiana, six weeks after a multibillion-dollar ground-up campus in Austin, Texas. Neither project made construction trade press as a "data center"-scale story, but together they point at something GCs and industrial subs should be tracking: as companies lean harder on automation and build more of their own robots, the plants that make those robots are becoming a distinct construction product — and it looks nothing like the warehouses those robots end up working in.

## What did Amazon actually announce?

On September 24, Amazon said it would invest more than $100 million to retrofit an existing 585,000-square-foot building at 1175 Collins Road in Greenwood, Indiana — across the street from one of its own fulfillment centers — into an advanced manufacturing plant. The facility will house fabrication, robotic welding, automated powder coating, and final assembly, coordinated by Amazon's cloud-based manufacturing systems. It's expected to open by 2028 and create roughly 300 jobs: CNC operators, welders, powder coating technicians, assemblers, controls engineers, quality assurance specialists, and manufacturing engineers, averaging close to $100,000 a year.

That comes six weeks after Amazon announced a very different kind of project: a multibillion-dollar, ground-up robotics manufacturing campus on roughly 300 acres in East Austin, Texas, expected to create 300 to 500 jobs. Together with two existing plants in Massachusetts, Greenwood becomes Amazon's fourth US site dedicated to building the robots — now more than a million of them — that move product through its fulfillment network.

## Retrofit or ground-up — why does that split matter to a contractor?

Because they're not the same scope of work, even though both plants make the same product line.

| | Greenwood, IN | Austin, TX |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery method | Retrofit of an existing 585,000 sq ft building | Ground-up construction on ~300 acres |
| Announced investment | $100 million-plus | Multibillion-dollar |
| Jobs | ~300 | 300–500 |
| Construction scope | Building shell reuse; new power, process utilities, floor systems, equipment fit-out | Full site development, structure, envelope, and process infrastructure |
| Timeline pressure | Opens by 2028 | Longer campus build cycle |

A retrofit like Greenwood is closer to heavy industrial tenant improvement work: reusing the shell but rebuilding the guts — electrical service sized for robotic welding cells and CNC equipment, ventilation and emissions controls for powder-coat curing ovens, and floor slabs stiff enough to hold precision assembly and welding equipment still, not the wide-open, lighter-duty floor a fulfillment warehouse gets by with. A ground-up campus like Austin carries the fuller civil, structural, and utility scope of any large industrial build, on a schedule set by a corporate manufacturing target rather than a typical spec-industrial timeline.

## Is this really a new construction vertical, or just more warehouse work?

It's neither. It's closer to what data centers looked like a few years ago: a physical building type driven by a company's internal automation strategy rather than standard industrial demand, growing fast enough that the delivery model itself is now splitting into distinct segments — the same fork the data center market went through as hyperscalers moved from one-off custom campuses toward faster, more repeatable builds. Robotics manufacturing plants are following that same path, just earlier and smaller. Amazon isn't the only company scaling domestic robot production, and each new plant announcement is effectively an RFP signal for a building type most industrial GCs haven't priced before: heavier power and process-utility loads than a warehouse, but without the scale or security requirements of a hyperscale data center.

## What should a GC or trade sub actually do with this?

If a robotics manufacturer's plant announcement lands in your market, don't price it off your warehouse or light-industrial cost book. Ask up front whether it's a retrofit or a ground-up build — that single fact changes whether your scope leans toward demolition, structural reinforcement, and utility upgrades, or toward full site development. Then get specific on process loads early: electrical service for welding and CNC lines, and ventilation and emissions handling for coating operations, are the line items most likely to be underscoped by a team that's used to bidding distribution centers.
