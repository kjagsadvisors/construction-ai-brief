Google says its first Wyoming data center will be air-cooled and powered largely by on-site, gas-fueled fuel cells with carbon capture. For mechanical and electrical subs, that combination changes which scopes exist on the job, and the owner has not yet said who will build them.

## What did Google announce?

In a [Nov. 5 post](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/infrastructure-and-cloud/global-network/wyoming-data-center/), Google committed $17 billion to Wyoming, centered on Laramie County near Cheyenne. The power plan is a 702 MW fuel cell project contracted with AEP, using Bloom Energy fuel cells that run on natural gas. Google calls it the largest planned development of its kind. Captured CO2 would be stored at Tallgrass Energy's nearby Eastern Wyoming Sequestration Hub. Google also lists gas generators run by Tallgrass and Black Hills Energy, and says the facility will be air-cooled to reduce water use.

Google was identified as the campus owner earlier this year, according to [Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2026/07/08/google-revealed-as-owner-of-wyomings-largest-data-center-now-project-tembo/). The post gives no construction timeline, no job count, and no breakdown of how the $17 billion splits between buildings and power.

## What does this mean for mechanical contractors?

Two things in the announcement touch Division 23 and Division 22 work directly:

- **Air cooling.** No cooling towers means no makeup water and condenser water loops in the usual sense. Heat rejection moves to dry coolers or air-cooled chillers, which tend to be physically larger and heavier for the same load. Expect more rooftop or yard-mounted equipment, rigging and structural coordination, and long equipment lead times.
- **Natural gas.** Fuel cells that run on gas need gas service, regulators, piping and pressure testing. On a typical building job, gas piping is a modest line. On a campus with its own generation, it is a major package, and code and utility requirements differ from standard building gas.

Whether that work sits with the GC, a separate power developer, or the equipment vendor is not public.

## What does this mean for electrical subs?

On-site generation changes the electrical one-line. Fuel cell output has to be collected, protected and tied into the campus distribution and, in most designs, the grid. That means switchgear, protection settings, metering and controls that must be tested together. Our [coverage of OpenAI's Grid Protectors program](/posts/2026-11-03-openai-grid-protectors-1000mw-curtailment-construction-electrical-controls-sub-flexible-load-scope) covered a similar point: when the campus generates or sheds power, controls and commissioning become bid-level items.

## Should a sub chase this work now?

Not on this announcement alone. Here is what we would check before spending estimating hours:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who is the contracting party for the power plant vs. the data halls? | Determines whether you bid to a GC, an owner or a power developer |
| Is the fuel cell work equipment-vendor scope or site-contractor scope? | Vendors often self-perform or use preferred installers |
| What is the schedule and phasing? | Google gave none; a first phase could be small |
| Who carries gas, carbon-capture and permitting risk? | Permitting delays can push out start dates and your payment timing |

The carbon-capture and sequestration piece is separate infrastructure and, as described, largely outside a typical building trade's scope.

## What is still unknown?

Most of what a sub needs: the GC or construction manager, bid packages, phasing, and the dollar split. One project database lists a Tembo carbon-storage facility at [carbonstorage.io](https://carbonstorage.io/facilities/project-tembo), but we have not independently confirmed its figures, so we do not rely on them here.

## The takeaway

If you are a mechanical or electrical sub with large-equipment rigging, gas piping or medium-voltage experience, put Laramie County on your watch list and ask your GC contacts who is buying the power plant packages. If you do not do that work, the practical read is narrower: air-cooled data centers shift mechanical dollars toward heat-rejection equipment and away from water systems, and that will show up in other owners' bids too.
