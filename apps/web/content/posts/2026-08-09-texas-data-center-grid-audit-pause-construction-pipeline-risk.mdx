Texas Governor Greg Abbott ordered state regulators to audit every data center in the grid's interconnection queue before approving another one to connect. Nothing new gets a grid connection until that audit clears it — and BloombergNEF says the pause already has roughly 49.8 gigawatts of US data center capacity, about a fifth of the entire national construction pipeline, sitting at risk of delay.

## What did Texas actually order, and when?

Abbott's directive, issued August 3, tells the Public Utility Commission of Texas and ERCOT to verify every project already moving through ERCOT's interconnection process — before either agency clears a new one. Developers have to disclose projected annual and peak electricity draw, water use, any planned on-site generation, and whether the project is paying its own way or leaning on state or local tax incentives, grants, or abatements. A project that doesn't supply that information can be denied a grid connection outright. It follows a June directive from Abbott requiring data centers to fully fund the grid infrastructure built to serve them, aimed at keeping those costs off residential ratepayers' bills.

The scale of what's being audited is the reason for the order: ERCOT's interconnection queue holds roughly 474 gigawatts of requests, about 90% of it from data centers, which utility regulators have said is more than five times Texas's record peak electricity demand. Oncor, the state's largest transmission utility, is now reviewing what the freeze means for a load pipeline of its own that runs to nearly 300 GW.

## How many construction projects does this actually touch?

A lot, and not just in Texas. ERCOT missed its own August 7 deadline to classify large loads under its "Batch Zero" interconnection study — the process that sorts which projects get priority grid access — and suspended those notifications while the audit runs. ERCOT is now asking the Public Utility Commission for a formal exception at its August 20 open meeting. Every project waiting on that classification stays parked until at least then, with no firm date for when it moves again.

## Is this just a Texas problem?

No — it's the leading edge of a pattern. New York got there first, with Governor Hochul signing an executive order in July pausing state environmental permits for any data center of 50 megawatts or larger, for up to a year, while the state writes new rules on grid, water, and community impact.

| Where | Action | When | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Texas | PUCT/ERCOT ordered to audit all interconnection requests | Aug. 3, 2026 | ~474 GW queue, ~90% data centers |
| New York | Statewide permit pause (first in the US) | Jul. 14, 2026 | Data centers ≥ 50 MW, up to 1 year |
| 14 states | Moratorium bills introduced | Early 2026 | Varies by state |
| Multiple cities/counties | Local pauses (Seattle, Spokane, Broomfield CO, Prince George's Co. MD, Sarasota Co. FL, others) | 2026 | Local zoning/permitting |

Data Center Watch, which tracks local opposition, counted at least 75 US data center projects worth roughly $130 billion blocked or delayed in the first quarter of 2026 alone — matching the pace of all of 2025 in three months — and put the number of active anti-data-center groups nationally at 833, up from 396 at the end of last year.

## What should a GC or sub with data center work in the pipeline do now?

1. **Check where your project sits in the queue, not just whether it has a permit.** A signed contract and an issued permit don't mean the interconnection is secure. If the project is in Texas, confirm whether it fell inside the paused Batch Zero classification before you commit crews or place long-lead orders against it.
2. **Reread the force majeure and regulatory-delay clauses before mobilizing further.** A law firm advising developers on the Texas order called it a "delay of indeterminate duration" — that's a different risk profile than a weather delay or a supply-chain lead time, and standard boilerplate may not address it cleanly.
3. **Track the state list, not just your own state.** Fourteen states have moratorium bills in play and the count is rising. If your backlog depends on data center work, that's now a bid-qualification question worth asking before you price the job, not after.

The honest read: the data center boom that's been stretching generator lead times and switchgear deliveries for the past year hasn't slowed down — but the regulators standing between a signed contract and a live grid connection just started pushing back. For a GC or sub counting on that backlog, the risk isn't the equipment anymore. It's whether the project gets permission to plug in at all.
