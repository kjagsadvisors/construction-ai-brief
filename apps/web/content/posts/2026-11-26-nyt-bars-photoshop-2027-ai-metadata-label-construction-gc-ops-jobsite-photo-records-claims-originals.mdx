The New York Times has told its freelance photographers not to use Photoshop 2027 on assignments, because the new AI features can leave metadata marking a photo as AI-edited, sometimes without the photographer realizing an AI tool ran. Contractors have the same problem with a different audience. A progress photo, a water damage shot or a picture of an unready area can end up as evidence in a delay claim or a backcharge dispute, and a hidden "AI-edited" tag is something the other side can point to.

## What did the Times actually say?

According to [PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/11/24/new-york-times-prohibits-photographers-from-using-photoshop-2027/), the Times emailed contributors telling them to avoid Photoshop 2027, version 28.0.0 and later updates, on Times work. A spokesperson said some functions "utilize generative AI in ways that may not be apparent to users of the software." The paper still supports Adobe's photo provenance standard. Its worry is that a photographer uses an AI feature without knowing it, the metadata records it, and the paper can no longer use the photo.

The new version adds or expands several AI tools, [per reporting summarized by AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/nyt-tells-contributing-photographers-to-skip-photoshop-2027):

- Generative Remove and Generative Expand
- Relighting and restyling
- AI upscaling
- An AI tool that recomposes an image to fit a different canvas size
- A step-by-step AI assistant

Adobe's own [help page](https://helpx.adobe.com/photoshop/using/content-credentials.html) says Photoshop attaches Content Credentials, a signed record of how an image was made and changed, when generative tools are used and the file is exported or saved as a copy. Adobe did not respond to PetaPixel before publication.

## Why does this matter on a jobsite?

Most GCs don't run Photoshop on field photos. But someone in the office usually does: the project engineer brightening a dark mechanical room shot for the owner report, the marketing coordinator cropping a crane out of a progress photo, the PM removing a sticky note from a picture of a marked-up wall. Upscaling or "remove" on a record photo is the risky case.

Photos do different jobs on a project, and they need different rules:

| Photo use | Who uses it | Can it be edited? |
|---|---|---|
| Delay, disruption or unready-area documentation | PM, claims consultant | No. Keep the camera original |
| Damage and backcharge records | Super, PM | No. Annotate a copy, never the original |
| Pay app and progress backup | Project engineer | Crop or brighten a copy only |
| Owner monthly report | PM | Edited copy, labeled as edited |
| Website and proposals | Marketing | Edit freely, but never from the record folder |

The Times rule makes the point a claims consultant would make anyway: whoever reviews a photo later will look at the file, not just the picture. AI provenance labels make that easier to do.

## What should a project team do this week?

1. **Find out who has Photoshop 2027.** Ask anyone with a Creative Cloud license which version they're on. PetaPixel reports a rollback to 27.0 is available through the Creative Cloud app, though not to earlier versions.
2. **Lock the originals.** Photos that might support a claim go to a read-only project folder, or stay in the photo platform's original upload, before anyone touches them.
3. **Edit copies only, and name them.** Add "-edited" to the filename so nobody submits the wrong one.
4. **Tell the office which features count as AI.** Remove, Expand, relighting and upscaling are the obvious ones. As one observer quoted in the coverage put it, it's not always clear which Adobe tools use AI.

## What are the limits?

We don't know yet how courts, arbitrators or insurers will treat an AI provenance label on a construction photo. A brightness tweak and an object removed with Generative Remove are very different edits, and the metadata may not tell them apart clearly. Adobe's documentation and user reports also disagree on when the credentials are switched on, so check your own install rather than trusting a setting you haven't seen.

We wrote earlier about [how thin the evidence is behind AI jobsite safety tools](/posts/2026-11-01-ai-jobsite-safety-evidence-thin-fyld-amey-construction-gc-pilot-metrics). Photo records have the same need: you have to be able to show where the data came from.

**Takeaway:** Before the next owner meeting, make a rule that the camera original of any photo that could support a claim never gets opened in an editing app. Edits happen on a labeled copy. That costs nothing, and it settles the question before an opposing expert raises it.
