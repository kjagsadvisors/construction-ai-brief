Six large U.S. banks now say that if you hand an AI agent your online banking login, whatever it does is on you, and at Citi that includes what a hacker does if the AI tool leaks your credentials. For a mechanical or electrical sub whose office manager is testing an agent to pay supplier invoices or reconcile the operating account, that settles one question. Until you've read your own bank agreement, no AI agent gets the bank login.

## What did the banks change?

Per [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/18/more-banks-says-customers-using-ai-agents-are-on-their-own-even-if-they-get-hacked/), Citi revised its Online User Agreement this week. Customers who give access to third-party AI tools are "responsible for activities conducted through those agents." Citizens emailed customers saying much the same, PNC changed its terms on November 18, and JPMorgan and Bank of America announced similar policies last month. The banks also say they won't be responsible for reversing transactions an automated assistant makes, whether it meant to or not.

Here's where the large banks stand, based on the reporting so far:

| Bank | What's been reported |
|---|---|
| Citi | Customer is responsible for agent activity, including hacks through a leaked AI tool. Agents Citi provides itself are the exception. |
| Wells Fargo | Updated Online Access Agreement takes effect Nov. 24. Agent instructions are "deemed to have been initiated and authorized directly by you, without further verification." |
| Bank of America | Activity from AI access is the customer's responsibility. A human must approve money leaving the account. |
| JPMorgan Chase | Executives say customers who share credentials with an outside agent carry the risk. |
| PNC, Citizens | Terms or customer notices updated this week. Details are thin. |

Wells Fargo's [Section 15](https://aimmediahouse.com/banking/wells-fargo-just-made-customers-liable-for-their-ai-agents) defines an AI agent as "any software that takes autonomous, semi-autonomous, or non-autonomous action on behalf of any person or entity." That's broad enough to cover a browser agent, a desktop assistant or a homemade script.

## Why does this matter to a trade sub now?

In the same week, the tools that could log into a bank portal got easier to switch on. Google [extended Gemini's computer use to the Mac](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/google-gives-gemini-the-ability-to-use-your-mac-for-you/) for paid U.S. users, and Microsoft put its always-on [Copilot Autopilot agent](https://siliconangle.com/2026/11/18/at-ignite-2026-microsoft-reinvents-copilot-as-the-autonomous-engine-of-enterprise-work/) into early access. A controller who wants to stop keying 40 supplier payments a week can now point an agent at a browser and walk away. The bank has just said, in writing, who owns the result.

There's a gap to be honest about. None of the coverage says whether these terms reach business or treasury accounts. They describe consumer online banking. Don't read that as a loophole. The federal consumer protections mentioned in the coverage come from Regulation E, which [defines an account](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/2/) as one "established primarily for personal, family, or household purposes." Your operating account isn't one of those, so it lives on whatever your commercial agreement says. Plenty of small subs also run some company bills through an owner's personal account or card, and that money falls under exactly these new consumer terms.

## What should the office do this week?

1. **Ask who has given an AI tool a bank login.** Check the office manager, the controller, the owner and any bookkeeper or outside accountant. Include browser extensions and "connect your bank" features in budgeting or AP apps.
2. **Pull the business banking or treasury agreement** and search it for "agent," "third party," "credentials" and "automated." If it says nothing, email your banker and get the answer in writing.
3. **Keep agents on the accounting side of the line.** An agent can read supplier invoices, match them to POs and receiving tickets, flag price changes against the quote, and stage a payment batch in your accounting system. A person with their own login releases the batch in the bank portal.
4. **Use the bank's own controls.** Dual approval on ACH and wires, Positive Pay, and per-user limits are already in most business banking packages. They also catch the ordinary fraud a sub sees, like a changed remit-to address on a supplier invoice.
5. **Rotate the password** on any bank login that has ever been pasted into an AI tool.

## What's still unclear?

How the banks will apply these terms to small business accounts, and whether bank-approved agent connections will arrive soon enough to make this moot. Citi already exempts agents it provides itself. A bank-sanctioned, read-only connection for reconciliation would be a very different risk from an agent holding the owner's password.
