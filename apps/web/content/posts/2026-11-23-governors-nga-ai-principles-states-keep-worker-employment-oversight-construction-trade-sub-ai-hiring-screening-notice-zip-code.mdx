The country's governors told Congress on November 22 that states should keep authority over how AI is used, including what it does to workers. For a mechanical or electrical sub hiring in several states, the practical read is simple: if you use AI to screen applicants, rank them or flag crew members for discipline, assume you will be answering to state rules for the foreseeable future, not one federal standard. One state already has a notice requirement on the books.

## What did the governors actually propose?

A bipartisan council of the [National Governors Association](https://www.nga.org/news/press-releases/governors-call-for-ai-action/), led by Maryland Gov. Wes Moore with Indiana's Mike Braun, North Carolina's Josh Stein and Utah's Spencer Cox, released six principles for the incoming Congress. The key split: "The federal government should address national and international risks as AI is developed," while states keep flexibility to govern AI as it is deployed.

Workers get their own principle. "Job creation must be a core priority as AI is rolled out across the economy," it says, and Moore said governors will focus on "making sure that workers have a voice in deployment of new technology." [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/governors-call-for-federal-ai-rules-without-sidelining-states), as summarized by [Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/governors-ask-congress-for-one-ai-rulebook-that-leaves-state-laws-standing/), reported that the plan leaves consumer protection, anti-discrimination enforcement and oversight of AI's effects on workers and employment with the states.

That puts the governors at odds with the White House. Its March framework asked Congress to preempt state AI laws that impose "undue burdens," and an executive order signed in December 2025 told the Justice Department to set up a task force to challenge state AI laws. No bill combining the two approaches has been published, and the fight is expected to pick up again in January.

## Why does this matter to a trade sub?

Because AI is already in the hiring and HR tools subs use, often without anyone deciding to buy "AI." Job boards rank applicants. Applicant tracking systems score resumes. Some jobsite camera and telematics products flag unsafe behavior that can end up in a write-up. Each of those can count as AI used in an employment decision.

Here is where the state rules stand, per [Ogletree](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/illinois-steps-up-ai-regulation-in-employment-key-takeaways-for-employers/) and [Eightfold's legal roundup](https://eightfold.ai/blog/ai-hiring-laws-nyc-illinois-colorado-eu/):

| State | Status | What it asks of employers |
|---|---|---|
| Illinois | In force since January 1, 2026 | Notify candidates and employees when AI is used in recruitment, hiring, promotion, discipline or discharge. No AI that discriminates by protected class, and no zip codes as a proxy. Applies with even one employee in the state. Implementing rules were withdrawn in June 2026, so notice format is still unsettled. |
| Colorado | Replacement law, SB 26-189, targeted for January 1, 2027 | Advance notice before covered automated decisions, a plain-language explanation within 30 days of an adverse decision, and a right to ask for correction and human reconsideration. Legal challenges are expected. |

## What should a sub do now?

1. **List every tool that touches hiring or discipline.** Job board ranking, resume scoring, video interview platforms, safety camera alerts that feed write-ups. Ask each vendor in writing whether the product uses AI to score or rank people.
2. **Check your address filters.** Screening out applicants who live far from the shop sounds harmless. Illinois bars zip codes as a proxy for protected classes, so a distance filter on an AI screen deserves a second look.
3. **Write one notice and use it where required.** If you hire anyone in Illinois, add a plain statement to your application and onboarding paperwork that says which steps use AI.
4. **Keep a person on the final call.** Let the tool sort, but have a foreman or office manager make and record the hire, fire or discipline decision.
5. **Watch January.** If Congress passes preemption, some of this could change. Until a bill passes, state law governs.

## What's still unclear?

Whether Congress sides with the governors or the White House, how Illinois will define acceptable notice, and whether Colorado's date holds. None of that changes the low-cost steps above.

**The takeaway:** the governors just signaled that AI rules for hiring and managing workers will keep coming from the states. If your office uses software to sort applicants or flag crews, find out this month which of those tools use AI, and make sure a person signs off on every decision about someone's job.
