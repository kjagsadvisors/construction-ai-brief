Anthropic's IPO prospectus says delays in securing power could slow its AI buildout, and that it may pay for grid upgrades even if the power capacity is delayed or never realized. For electrical and sitework subs on data center jobs, that is a direct warning: the utility's timeline, not your crew, may set the schedule, so your subcontract needs to say who pays when it slips.

## What did Anthropic actually say?

According to [Semafor's reporting on the filing](https://www.semafor.com/article/10/27/2026/anthropic-says-energy-difficulties-could-slow-down-the-ai-buildout), Anthropic told potential investors that:

- its already-high energy costs will likely rise;
- delays in securing power could slow the AI buildout;
- it is committed to about $400 billion in chip purchases and data center leases;
- it cites negotiations with third parties, delays, cost overruns and growing requirements for AI companies to pay for grid upgrades, even ones they don't use directly;
- it "may bear these costs even if the underlying [power] capacity is delayed or never realized."

A separate [daily AI roundup](https://www.theneuron.ai/digest/everything-that-happened-in-ai-today-wednesday-october-28-2026/) pairs this with a Washington Post estimate that AI companies could seek roughly $10 trillion for chips and data centers through 2032. We could not open the Post piece, so treat that figure as reported, not verified.

This is a risk-factor section, which lawyers write defensively. It is not a forecast that projects will stall. But it is a public statement from one of the largest AI buyers that power access is a schedule and cost risk it cannot control.

## Why does this matter to a trade sub?

Because the owner may be paying for things that never energize. If a tenant or developer is on the hook for grid upgrades regardless of whether power arrives, a late utility date puts pressure on everyone downstream: finished switchgear sits idle, crews are held or resequenced, and somebody gets asked to absorb it.

Where the pressure can land on you:

| Risk | How it shows up | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Utility energization slips | Gear installed but can't be commissioned; retainage and final billing stall | Is utility delay an excusable, compensable delay? |
| Owner re-sequences | Mobilize, demobilize, remobilize | Standby and remobilization rates in the contract |
| Owner trims scope to save cash | Phases deferred or cancelled | Termination-for-convenience payment terms |
| Pressure to accelerate | Overtime to hit a fixed power date | Whether acceleration is paid or "directed" |

## What should an electrical or sitework sub check before bidding?

1. **Excusable delays.** Does the list include utility and interconnection delays, or only "force majeure"? Many forms are vague here.
2. **Compensation for delay.** Excusable and compensable are different. Time without money still costs you crew and bonding capacity.
3. **Notice deadlines.** Delay claims often die on missed notice. Log every utility date change the day you learn of it.
4. **Long-lead gear.** Transformers and switchgear are bought early. Ask who owns storage, insurance and payment if the energization date moves.
5. **Payment terms.** Pay-when-paid or pay-if-paid clauses tied to an owner who is cash-constrained are a bigger risk than the work itself.

## What's still unknown?

Plenty. The filing doesn't name projects, utilities or dates, and nothing in it says a specific job is late. Anthropic is one buyer, and other owners may be better positioned on power. We also don't know how much of any grid-upgrade cost will flow to builders versus stay with owners. Don't re-price a bid off a risk-factor paragraph.

**Takeaway:** On your next data center or other large-load bid, ask the GC one question in writing: what is the utility's committed energization date, and who pays if it moves? If the answer is "we'll sort it out later," price the standby, or pass.
