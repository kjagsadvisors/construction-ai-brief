The FCC added Chinese-made quadruped and humanoid robots, along with foreign-made connected power inverters, to its national-security Covered List on July 28, 2026. New models in either category can no longer get the FCC equipment authorization needed to be imported, marketed, or sold in the US, effective immediately, with no grandfather period for anything not yet authorized. Nothing you already own is affected. Anything you're about to order might be.

## What exactly changed?

The Covered List is the same mechanism that got DJI drones blocked from new US sales in December 2025. The FCC's notice this week expanded it to cover two new categories: "advanced robotic devices" — its term for mobile robots, humanoid and quadruped alike — and "connected power inverters produced in foreign countries." The rationale, per the FCC's own notice, is that "the networked capabilities of advanced robotic systems create extensive vulnerabilities and vectors for attacks that can manipulate the data and physical operation of the advanced robotic system." The restriction only touches new equipment authorizations. A robot or inverter model that already cleared FCC review before July 28 keeps selling. A model that hasn't is stuck.

## Does this touch the robot dogs already doing inspection walks?

We flagged this exact mechanism three weeks ago, when Unitree's $619 million Shanghai IPO landed next to two bills in Congress proposing to do precisely this. Neither bill has passed. The FCC did it anyway, administratively, without waiting for a floor vote.

The security case behind it isn't theoretical. Unitree's Go1 quadruped shipped with a firmware backdoor (CVE-2025-2894) — a cloud tunnel service called CloudSail that connected back to a Chinese vendor and handed remote control of the robot to anyone holding the right API key. Researchers eventually traced nearly 1,900 vulnerable Go1 units still connected worldwide, including devices reached from inside MIT, Princeton, and Carnegie Mellon networks. That's the pattern the FCC cited in going after the broader robotic-device category, not just Unitree by name.

For a GC already running a quadruped for progress capture or LiDAR scanning, the immediate exposure is narrow: existing units and already-authorized models keep working. The exposure shows up the next time you order a new one.

## What about the inverter going into your data center's power scope?

This is the less obvious hit, and it lands on a workflow the Brief has covered repeatedly this month: data centers and other large loads increasingly bring co-located solar and battery storage into their own power scope, in part because of the grid strain PJM and other operators are already flagging. Huawei and Sungrow are the two largest solar inverter manufacturers in the world by shipment volume. Both are Chinese. Any new order for one of their models that hasn't already cleared FCC equipment authorization can no longer legally be imported or connected — not delayed, blocked.

| If you're procuring... | What to check now |
|---|---|
| A new inspection or reality-capture robot | Ask the vendor for the specific model's FCC ID and confirm it was authorized before July 28, 2026 |
| A solar or battery-storage inverter for any project | Confirm the exact model number already holds FCC equipment authorization before you commit it to a submittal or a schedule |
| Either category, mid-procurement | Ask whether the manufacturer is pursuing a Conditional Approval exemption through DoD or DHS, and get a realistic timeline in writing — don't assume it clears in time for your schedule |

## What to do with this now

None of this claws back equipment already on a jobsite or already authorized. The exposure is entirely forward-looking: the next robot you add to a fleet, the next inverter you specify for a solar-plus-storage scope. Both are decisions that usually get made weeks before delivery, which is exactly the window where an FCC ID check costs nothing and a missed one costs a schedule slip.
