AI chatbots pointed shoppers to fake, counterfeit or dead retail sites in about 9% of where-to-buy answers in a new test, and up to 15% for the worst performer. For a trade sub, the practical rule is short: an AI assistant can help identify a part, but it should not be how anyone in your shop decides which website gets the company card.

## What did the study find?

Podqi, a brand-protection firm, asked ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity where to buy products from 151 brands during September 2026, including whether a brand ships internationally. The Verge first reported the results on November 12. The figures below are from [Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/podqi-study-finds-chatgpt-gemini-and-perplexity-send-shoppers-to-fake-stores/) and [Digit](https://www.digit.in/news/general/shopping-with-ai-chatgpt-gemini-and-other-ai-chatbots-can-lead-you-to-fake-websites-study-finds.html).

| Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Answers collected | 6,795 |
| Domains reviewed | 4,437 |
| Domains flagged as risky | 718 (351 fake, 367 dead) |
| Answers containing a risky site, overall | About 9% |
| Perplexity | 15% |
| ChatGPT | 8% |
| Gemini | 4% |
| Risky answers where the chatbot actively suggested the site | 57% |
| Times ChatGPT flagged a risky site as unreliable | 24% |

Podqi counted a site as risky if it impersonates a retailer, sells counterfeits, is an abandoned domain someone else took over, or is a dead domain a fraudster could register. Risky sites were recommended 6.3 times as often as they were warned against. In one case, Perplexity named the wrong page as the official Stüssy site and described it as a source of authentic products.

Two patterns matter more than the headline numbers:

- **Smaller brands had roughly double the exposure** of large ones.
- **Regional storefront questions were the worst category at 16%.** The chatbots sometimes produced a plausible regional site for a brand that has none.

## Does this apply to construction purchasing?

Not directly, and we will not pretend otherwise. Podqi tested apparel and housewares brands such as Lululemon, Birkenstock and Le Creuset. Nobody measured breakers, valve actuators or cordless tool batteries.

But look at where the chatbots failed: small brands, and "does this company sell here" questions. That is a fair description of construction parts hunting. A service tech needs a control board for a discontinued rooftop unit. A foreman needs a replacement part from a specialty manufacturer with a thin web presence. A purchasing agent is checking who stocks a specific fitting line. Those are small-brand, find-me-a-seller questions, and they are the kind a person now types into a chatbot instead of a search engine.

The cost of a bad answer is also higher on a job than in a closet. A shopper who lands on a fake store loses a card number. A sub that installs a counterfeit electrical or life-safety component has a product that does not match the approved submittal, with the warranty and liability questions that follow.

## How much should you trust these numbers?

Some, with caveats:

- Podqi sells brand-protection services, so it benefits from the finding.
- The test covered one month and 151 brands. It is a snapshot.
- None of the three companies disputed the problem or offered competing data. Google said Gemini links to sources so users can verify. OpenAI said it is improving safeguards against fake or misleading results. Perplexity acknowledged its AI is not always fully accurate.

## What should a sub change this week?

1. **Split identify from buy.** Using a chatbot to work out a part number, a cross-reference or a spec is reasonable. Choosing the seller is a separate step that runs through the manufacturer's site.
2. **Check the authorized distributor list.** Most manufacturers publish one. If the seller is not on it, call the manufacturer or your regular supply house before ordering.
3. **Read the domain.** Podqi CEO Trevor West's advice is to verify where a chatbot's information comes from before entering card details, and to watch for misspelled domains and unusual extensions.
4. **Run new vendors through vendor setup.** A site a chatbot suggested is a new vendor. It gets the same W-9 and approval steps as any other, even for a $200 part. The same discipline protects you from the [payment fraud we covered in October](/posts/2026-10-03-microsoft-defense-report-ai-phishing-construction-payment-fraud).
5. **Tell the field.** P-card holders and service techs are the ones most likely to do this from a phone in a mechanical room. One paragraph in the next toolbox talk or purchasing memo covers it.

**Takeaway:** The study does not prove construction supply is infested with fake sites. It shows the tools vouch for sellers they have not verified, most often for small brands. Keep the chatbot for figuring out what the part is, and keep your approved vendor list and the manufacturer's distributor page for deciding who you buy it from.
