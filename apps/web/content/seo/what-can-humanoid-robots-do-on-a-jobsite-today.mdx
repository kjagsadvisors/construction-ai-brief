One humanoid robot works on a live construction jobsite today — a stock Unitree G1 nicknamed "Douglas," running 360-degree progress-capture walks for UK contractor Tilbury Douglas since April 2026 — and even that one is only fully autonomous inside a single fenced test zone. Everywhere else on the site, it walks on a leash: a human handler beside it, one hand on an emergency stop. Every other humanoid shipping at real volume — Figure's Figure 03, Boston Dynamics' Atlas, Apptronik's Apollo 2, Agility Robotics' Digit — still works exclusively indoors, on a flat factory or warehouse floor.

## Is any humanoid robot actually deployed on a construction jobsite in 2026?

One, with real caveats. Tilbury Douglas became the first UK tier-one contractor to put a humanoid on a live site, deploying an off-the-shelf Unitree G1 — roughly £15,000, about $19,000 — running the contractor's own AI framework on top ([Tilbury Douglas](https://www.tilburydouglas.co.uk/tilbury-douglas-becomes-first-contractor-to-launch-a-humanoid-robot-on-construction-site/); [Construction News](https://www.constructionnews.co.uk/tech/tilbury-douglas-deploys-humanoid-robot-on-live-construction-site-21-04-2026/)). It's a genuine bipedal humanoid doing genuine outdoor site work — the first documented case of that combination. It is not, however, doing anything a quadruped like Boston Dynamics' Spot wasn't already doing: walking a fixed route and taking pictures.

## What is Douglas, and what does it actually do?

Autonomous data collection, not construction labor. Douglas navigates a set route to capture 360-degree imagery and detailed progress reports, photographing each area from the same position every visit so AI software can compare images over time and flag deviations automatically — the same before/after logic GCs already use with fixed jobsite cameras, just on legs that can climb stairs and step over debris ([Construction Enquirer](https://www.constructionenquirer.com/2026/04/21/humanoid-robot-goes-to-work-on-tilbury-douglas-site/)). Tilbury Douglas estimates it saves roughly 40 hours of administrative labor a month. The important limit: autonomy is currently confined to one fenced test area at a Birmingham site. On every other site Douglas visits during its planned 12-month UK tour, it's accompanied by a handler with an emergency stop — Tilbury Douglas's own answer to the safety-certification gap covered below ([Industrialised Construction](https://www.industrialisedconstruction.co.uk/2026/05/22/tilbury-douglas-trials-humanoid-robot-on-live-construction-site/)).

## What are the other humanoid robots doing, if not construction?

Still sorting and material handling, on floors that never change shape:

- **Figure 03 (Figure AI)** — Still running sequencing work at BMW's Spartanburg, South Carolina plant, picking components into a trolley for the assembly line ([The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/bmw-group-deploys-figure-03-humanoid-after-tests-previous-version/)). In August 2026, Figure released video of Figure 03 autonomously climbing and descending a 15-foot ladder — a genuinely construction-relevant capability — but the company hasn't disclosed the underlying technical details, and the demo hasn't been independently verified by a third party or shown outside a controlled setting ([Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/figures-new-humanoid-scales-ladder-autonomously-marking-ai-mobility-advancement)).
- **Apollo 2 (Apptronik)** — Apptronik replaced its original Apollo with Apollo 2 in June 2026, now offered in bipedal or wheeled-base configurations, and closed a Series A that grew past $935 million with Mercedes-Benz, Google, and John Deere among the backers ([Apptronik](https://apptronik.com/news-collection/apptronik-closes-over-935-million-series-a); [Vestbee](https://www.vestbee.com/insights/articles/935-m-series-a-for-apptronik)). Apptronik itself calls Apollo 2 a training and data-collection platform, not a finished product — it's pointing to Apollo 3, targeted for 2027, as its first true commercial release.
- **Digit (Agility Robotics)** — Still the most mature deployment by operating hours: over 65,000 hours across customer sites including Amazon, GXO Logistics, and Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada, all indoors ([Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/digit-humanoid-robot-moves-100000-totes)). Agility is going public via a SPAC merger with Churchill Capital Corp XI, expected to close by the end of 2026 and trade as "AGLT" — a warehouse-and-logistics track record funding the next round of R&D, not a construction pivot ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/digit-maker-agility-robotics-to-go-public-in-2-5b-deal-heres-what-the-filings-say-about-its-finances/)).

Every one of those deployments still shares the traits an open jobsite doesn't have: a flat floor, a fixed layout, and low task variability.

## What's stopping a humanoid from working a full jobsite unsupervised?

The same two problems as a year ago — and Douglas's own deployment is the clearest proof yet that neither is solved. First, navigation: every deployed humanoid still follows a pre-mapped route or a human joystick, and a jobsite's walkable path changes weekly as framing and scaffolding move. Mistral's Robostral Navigate, a map-free navigation model that steers a robot through unfamiliar space with one ordinary camera, has been available since July 2026, but Mistral names manufacturing, delivery, logistics, and hospitality as its target uses — not construction — and no vendor has published a field test of it on real jobsite conditions ([Mistral AI](https://mistral.ai/news/robostral-navigate/)). Second, safety certification: NVIDIA launched Halos for Robotics in June 2026, the first accredited safety-inspection framework for physical AI, but it was built and validated for warehouse and factory settings ([NVIDIA / GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/06/22/3315323/0/en/NVIDIA-Announces-Halos-for-Robotics-the-Industry-s-First-Full-Stack-Safety-System-for-Physical-AI.html)). Nothing equivalent exists yet for an unstructured, occupied outdoor site — which is exactly why Tilbury Douglas still pairs Douglas with a human handler and an e-stop everywhere outside its one certified test zone.

## Should a GC care that Douglas runs on Unitree hardware specifically?

Yes — treat the brand's momentum and its security record as two separate questions. Unitree priced a Shanghai STAR Market IPO on August 19, 2026 at roughly a $9 billion valuation, with shares popping more than 500% on debut ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/19/china-backflipping-robot-maker-unitree-jumps-shanghai-ipo.html)). Weeks before that, the FCC added Unitree's robots to its Covered List over UniPwn, a wormable Bluetooth Low Energy exploit that grants root access to Go2, B2, G1, and H1 units from roughly 30 meters away, using a hardcoded encryption key shared across the product line ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/fcc-blocks-new-foreign-produced-robots.html); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/unitree-robot-exploit)). The G1 is the exact platform Douglas runs on, and no firmware patch had shipped as of this update. A successful IPO changes Unitree's cash position; it doesn't change the due-diligence questions a GC should ask before putting one of these units — humanoid or quadruped — anywhere near a live site network.

## Where's the realistic next landing spot for humanoids?

Two paths are now open, not one. The path most vendors have pitched — a purpose-built construction humanoid from Figure, Apptronik, or Agility — still points first to off-site modular and prefab plants, where a flat floor and fixed stations resemble the warehouse environments those robots already work in ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/engineering-construction-and-building-materials/our-insights/humanoid-robots-in-the-construction-industry-a-future-vision)). Douglas opened a second path: buy a general-purpose humanoid off the shelf and build your own narrow AI workflow on top of it, the way Tilbury Douglas did, rather than wait for a construction-specific product to ship. Built Robotics is running a parallel bet with non-humanoid hardware — a research partnership with Penn Engineering's xLab, announced in June 2026, aimed at a formal safety case for its autonomous survey machines working around people on active solar sites ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/built-robotics-and-penn-engineerings-xlab-partner-to-advance-physical-ai-in-construction-302802050.html)) — the same open safety problem, attacked from the equipment side instead of the humanoid side.

## The honest read

If a vendor pitches you a humanoid robot for your active jobsite this year, ask for the same thing Tilbury Douglas would tell you to ask: where, exactly, does the autonomy stop and the human handler with the e-stop begin? As of this update, the honest answer for every humanoid on the market is "almost immediately" outside one fenced test zone in Birmingham. What you can put to work today, unsupervised, is narrower and less glamorous — a quadruped running inspection walks, or the task-specific machines already earning their keep, covered in full in [our guide to construction robots and physical AI](/seo/construction-robots-jobsite-physical-ai-guide). Douglas is a real first step, not a finished product — watch whether Tilbury Douglas expands that fenced autonomous zone before you watch any vendor's trade-show stage.
