The contractor, engineer, or design professional who approved the AI-assisted work is liable when it turns out wrong — not the AI vendor. Vendor agreements are written specifically to prevent the alternative: liability caps that limit what you can recover from the software maker to a few months of license fees, no matter how large the resulting rework, delay, or injury claim actually is. If your firm is using AI anywhere in design, estimating, submittal review, or scheduling, you are the party holding the exposure, and the standard-form contracts your industry runs on don't say so explicitly yet.

That gap — real liability, no contract language addressing it — is why this question keeps coming up in 2026. Here's how liability actually splits by the type of mistake, what your insurance says right now, and what to put in writing before your next AI-touched deliverable becomes a dispute.

## Why can't I just blame the AI vendor?

Because you agreed not to when you clicked through the license. AI vendor contracts routinely cap the vendor's liability at a small fixed dollar amount or the cost of the software subscription itself — legal analysis of these agreements points to caps commonly set at roughly one to three months of fees — which creates a gap between what a bad AI output actually costs your project and what you can recover from the company that built the tool ([Dan Cumberland Labs, "The 8-Point AI Clause Audit for EPC Contracts"](https://dancumberlandlabs.com/blog/engineering-procurement-and-construction-contracts/)). Under the liability framework courts are applying, the "integrator" — the party that deployed the AI and benefited from its output — is the first party a claim reaches, ahead of the model developer, unless you can show the developer owed you a specific duty of care that most commercial license agreements are drafted to disclaim ([HFW, "Legal Liability for AI Decisions: Who Is Responsible When AI Fails?"](https://www.hfw.com/insights/legal-liability-for-ai-driven-decisions-when-ai-gets-it-wrong-who-can-you-turn-to/)). In practice, on almost every AI tool your firm runs, you are the integrator.

## Does liability change based on what kind of mistake the AI made?

Yes — legal exposure on a construction project sorts into three distinct categories, and AI can now be the root cause of any of them ([AWCI, "AI and Automation at Construction Sites"](https://www.awci.org/media/construction-trends/ai-and-automation-at-construction-sites-powerful-tools-or-new-source-of-liability)):

- **Personal injury or property damage** — an AI-enabled machine or jobsite camera system misreads a condition and someone gets hurt. This can pull in products-liability claims against the equipment manufacturer and the AI software vendor directly, on top of a standard negligent-supervision claim against whoever was operating or overseeing the equipment.
- **Construction defects** — an AI tool drafts a spec update, a takeoff, or a submittal comparison that's wrong, and it gets built. Liability lands on whoever approved the AI's output without catching the error, litigated as ordinary professional negligence or breach of the standard of care.
- **Delay damages** — an AI scheduling tool or document-review agent produces a bad output that costs time rather than causing physical harm, and the delay claim follows the contract's normal delay-damages allocation, with the AI's role as the contributing cause rather than a separate liability theory.

The category matters because it determines which policy, if any, responds — a products-liability claim, a professional-negligence claim, and a delay claim can each trigger different coverage, or none.

## What does "hallucinated" AI output actually look like in a construction document?

It's not hypothetical. AI tools used to update specification sections or harmonize requirements across projects can generate "phantom codes" — plausible-sounding standard designations that don't exist in any actual standards catalog, confident enough in tone that a rushed reviewer waves them through ([Got Law STL, "When 'Smart' Tools Make Dumb Mistakes"](https://gotlawstl.com/when-smart-tools-make-dumb-mistakes-ais-hidden-risks-in-construction-documents/)). The same failure mode has already reached construction litigation directly: in *A2K Inc. v. Fluor Federal Services*, a staffing subcontractor's attorneys used generative AI to draft and cite-check a legal brief in a construction-adjacent contracts dispute, and the filing allegedly included a fabricated quote attributed to a Texas Supreme Court decision that doesn't exist ([Jones Walker, "Fake AI Citations Strike in a Construction Lawsuit"](https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/perspectives/fake-ai-citations-strike-in-a-construction-lawsuit.html)). The lesson generalizes past legal filings: any AI-drafted document that cites a standard, a code section, or a manufacturer spec needs a named human checking that the citation is real before it becomes a project record.

## Does insurance actually cover an AI-caused mistake?

For now, often yes — but that's changing fast, and by carrier. Most professional liability and CGL policies written before 2026 still treat AI-assisted work as ordinary negligence with no special exclusion. That's ending: Verisk's new generative-AI exclusion endorsements took effect January 1, 2026, and several carriers have moved faster than the industry standard. Berkley introduced an absolute AI exclusion across its D&O, E&O, and fiduciary-liability products that names ChatGPT, Bard, Midjourney, and DALL-E specifically; Hamilton followed with its own generative-AI exclusion; Philadelphia Insurance and Hamilton Select went further and excluded AI-related claims from E&O entirely ([2026 E&O Renewal Audit Guide](https://norfolkdailynews.com/online_features/press_releases/ai-assisted-design-work-and-e-o-a-2026-renewal-audit-guide/article_ae88c181-68d9-504e-85d6-ad5a04ad4479.html)). A policy that covered your AI-assisted submittal review last year may not cover the same workflow at your next renewal.

## Comparison: who's actually on the hook, by scenario

| Scenario | Who's typically liable | What limits or shifts it |
|---|---|---|
| AI drafts a bad RFI response or spec update that gets built | The engineer, PE, or PM who approved it | Named human sign-off on every AI-assisted item, documented in the log |
| AI vendor's tool is objectively defective | Still mostly you, up to a point | Vendor's contractual liability cap (often ~1-3 months of fees) limits recovery from the vendor |
| Sub uses an AI tool the GC didn't specify | The sub, under standard indemnification language | An explicit AI carve-out in the subcontract, negotiated in advance |
| AI-enabled equipment causes a jobsite injury | Contractor (negligent supervision) + possibly equipment maker and AI vendor (products liability) | Documented training, maintenance, and human oversight of the AI-enabled system |
| Insurance claim traces to an AI-assisted deliverable | The firm carrying the policy, if the claim is denied | Written AI governance policy; confirming your 2026 renewal language before a claim, not after |

## What should a GC or sub actually do about this?

Three things, in order. First, put a named human sign-off on every AI-assisted deliverable that leaves your office — an RFI response, a submittal comparison, a spec update — so you have a documented standard-of-care trail if a dispute ever asks who approved what. Second, ask your broker directly whether your current E&O or CGL policy carries a generative-AI exclusion, and get that answer in writing before your next renewal, not during the renewal call. Third, don't wait for AIA or ConsensusDocs to update their standard forms — neither currently addresses AI-generated work product or vendor data rights, so the fix is a one-page side letter on your next contract specifying who verifies AI-assisted output and how an AI-caused dispute gets resolved ([ConsensusDocs, "Handling Responsibility for A.I. in Construction"](https://www.consensusdocs.org/news/handling-responsibility-for-a-i-in-construction/)).

None of this means slowing down AI adoption — it means making sure the paper trail exists before you need it.
