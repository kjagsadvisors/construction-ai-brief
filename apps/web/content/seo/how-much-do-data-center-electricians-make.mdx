Electricians doing data center work earn more than the $63,190 national median for the trade, but the size of the premium depends on how you measure it. Indeed Hiring Lab found a 42% median hourly premium in data center postings that listed hourly pay and 12% in postings with annual pay. Higher numbers — $94,500 medians, $150,000 senior packages, $260,000 outliers — circulate widely, and they come mostly from recruiters and local reporting, not from audited wage data.

Here's what the evidence supports, where the headlines overreach, and what it means for a sub pricing labor or defending a crew.

## What's the actual pay range for a data center electrician in 2026?

Start with the only authoritative anchor: the Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median electrician at **$63,190 a year ($30.38 an hour) as of May 2025** ([BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/electricians.htm)). Everything about data center pay is a premium on top of that.

Indeed Hiring Lab's July 2026 analysis, summarized by [Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/ai-data-center-boom-raises-electrician-pay-d83d97c5), found:

- **Hourly postings:** median premium of about 42% (roughly $10 an hour) over comparable non-data-center roles
- **Postings with annual pay:** median premium of 12%

Both measure *advertised* pay on postings that disclosed it. They don't capture overtime, per diem, or what people actually earned.

Above that, the numbers get softer. Recruiter-sourced reports put senior and commissioning electricians in the $125,000–$150,000 range, and traveling crews higher once overtime is stacked on ([iRecruit](https://www.irecruit.co/insights/data-center-construction-labor-market-report)). Outliers near $260,000 get repeated a lot, but they require sustained overtime, relocation, and an acute local shortage — not a standard 40-hour week ([Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2026/04/05/us-data-centers-drive-explosion-in-pay-for-electricians-some-as-much-as-260-000/)).

We'd plan around a base premium in the low-to-mid double digits and treat six-figure totals as an overtime-and-travel outcome.

## Why is data center electrician pay running ahead of standard commercial work?

Two forces stack. First, electrical scope is simply bigger: Associated Builders and Contractors CEO Michael Bellaman has put electrical at roughly half of labor on a typical data center, and IBEW estimates run 45% to 70% of total construction cost on these projects. Second, the work — redundant power architecture, UPS and switchgear commissioning, arc-flash-rated energized testing — sits outside standard commercial electrical work, which shrinks the pool that can do it on day one.

Bloomberg's July 2026 reporting quantifies the pressure: electrician wages up 4.6% year over year, journeyperson wages up 6.6%, apprentice wages up 8.2%, and commercial electrician wages up 9.9%, against 2.4% for the US workforce as a whole ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-08/labor-crunch-tests-growth-limits-for-us-data-center-builders)).

## How does union work compare with non-union?

Union scales and benefit packages generally run richer than non-union on comparable scope, and the gap widens once health and pension contributions are counted. We couldn't find an authoritative dataset isolating a data center-specific union premium, so we won't put a percentage on it. Pull the actual local scale and fringe package for your market before you bid.

## What does the shortage look like, and does it change the pay trajectory?

Industry reporting puts the data center construction workforce gap at up to 499,000 workers, with electricians the most acute category ([iRecruit](https://www.irecruit.co/insights/data-center-construction-labor-market-report)). ITIF's more conservative estimate is about 439,000 across all of construction, naming electricians and pipe layers as the tightest trades and data center growth as the driver ([ITIF](https://itif.org/publications/2026/01/12/construction-industry-facing-worker-shortage-driven-by-growth-of-data-centers/)). CSIS cites McKinsey's estimate that the 2023–2030 AI infrastructure buildout alone needs roughly 130,000 additional electricians ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/genais-human-infrastructure-challenge-can-united-states-meet-skilled-trade-labor-demand)).

Because it takes about four years to turn a recruit into a journeyman, the gap can't close on a normal hiring cycle. The newest national data fits that picture: AGC's read of October 2026 jobs numbers found construction's unemployment rate tied a record low for the month and wages rising faster than in other sectors, even as total construction employment dipped by 3,000 ([AGC](https://www.agc.org/news/2026/11/06/construction-employment-stalls-pool-unemployed-workers-dries-october-despite-sharp-rise-wages)). Fortune and CNBC frame it as a multi-year structural gap, with employers answering via signing bonuses and faster apprenticeship pipelines ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/02/ai-data-centers-electrician-shortage-gen-z-training-careers/); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/18/ai-data-center-buildout-jobs-salary-skilled-traders-worker-shortage.html)).

One caution: advertised pay is not guaranteed to keep rising. Posting-level data can shift as the mix of jobs changes, so benchmark against current local offers, not a headline.

## What should an electrical sub actually do with this data?

Benchmark retention against live local offers, not your bid history. A journeyman paid at standard commercial rates is a recruiting target for every data center GC in your market, and the four-year training lag means you can't hire your way out of a departure. If you want this work, price the labor premium into your rate before you bid — not after you lose the crew that would have staffed the job.
