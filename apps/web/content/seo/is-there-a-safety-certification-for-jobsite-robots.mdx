No. As of September 2026, no accredited, government-mandated safety certification exists for a robot working an active construction jobsite. The most concrete progress toward one — Agility Robotics' Digit passing a real field evaluation, then the September 15 launch of Digit 5 on NVIDIA's Halos safety stack — is real, but it's aimed at fulfillment centers and factory floors, not outdoor, unstructured jobsites. If a vendor tells you their robot is "safety certified," the honest follow-up question is: certified for what environment, against which standard, and by whom?

## What's the most concrete certification progress so far, and does it reach construction?

In November 2025, Agility's Digit became the first humanoid to pass a field evaluation by a Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory — an OSHA-recognized certifier — assessed against ANSI/RIA R15.08, ISO 13849, and ISO 12100. That's a real third-party safety evaluation, not a press claim. It happened on a live customer fulfillment line, the same kind of structured, indoor environment Digit has always worked in ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/agility-unveils-digit-5-humanoid-robot-built-for-cooperatively-safe-work-at-scale-302878540.html)).

Agility built on that with Digit 5, unveiled September 15, 2026: a new safety architecture running on NVIDIA's IGX Thor hardware and Halos software stack, designed to let the robot work next to people without the physical safety cage industrial robots have traditionally required. Agility is the first launch partner running a robot through NVIDIA's Halos AI Systems Inspection Lab, and the company says it's now a project leader contributing to both ANSI/A3 TR R15.108 (a new U.S./Canada technical report specifically for dynamically stable mobile robots, including humanoids) and ISO 25785-1 ([Robotics 24/7](https://www.robotics247.com/article/agility-robotics-debuts-digit-5-humanoid-robot-for-cooperatively-safe-work-at-scale); [RoboticsTomorrow](https://www.roboticstomorrow.com/news/2026/09/15/agility-robotics-unveils-digit-5-humanoid-robot-built-for-cooperatively-safe-work-at-scale/27092)). We covered [what Digit 5 means for a prefab shop here](/posts/2026-09-16-agility-robotics-digit-5-humanoid-cageless-prefab-shop-safety) — short version: Agility's own timeline runs through 2027, and every customer named (GXO, Schaeffler, Amazon, Toyota) is a warehouse or manufacturing floor. Nothing in the announcement points at an outdoor jobsite.

## What is NVIDIA Halos, and why does it matter here?

Halos for Robotics is NVIDIA's entry into robot safety certification: a combination of IGX Thor (industrial compute hardware with built-in safety mechanisms), Halos Core and Halos OS (the software layer), and an AI Systems Inspection Lab accredited by the ANSI National Accreditation Board — the first such program for physical AI ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/06/22/3315323/0/en/NVIDIA-Announces-Halos-for-Robotics-the-Industry-s-First-Full-Stack-Safety-System-for-Physical-AI.html)). The inspection lab is recognized by TÜV Rheinland, TÜV SÜD, UL Solutions, exida, SGS, and CertX — the certifying bodies that already approve industrial equipment used on construction projects — targeting IEC 61508 (functional safety), ISO 13849 (safety of machinery), and ISO/IEC TR 5469 (AI safety) ([HPCWire](https://www.hpcwire.com/aiwire/2026/06/23/nvidia-announces-halos-for-robotics-the-industrys-first-full-stack-safety-system-for-physical-ai/)). Agility's Digit is the first robot moving through that lab. Still no construction customer or outdoor use case has been named for any Halos-certified deployment. We [covered the original Halos announcement in full here](/posts/2026-06-24-nvidia-halos-robot-safety-certification-jobsite) when it broke.

## Why doesn't a warehouse-grade certification just carry over to a jobsite?

Because the standards Halos and Digit's field evaluation target weren't written for the terrain most likely to matter on your site. ANSI/RIA R15.08 governs industrial mobile robots working around people on a structured factory or warehouse floor. ISO 10218:2025 and ANSI/A3 R15.06-2025 — the U.S. industrial robot safety standard, expanded to more than 370 pages across three parts and updated in October 2025 ([Automate.org](https://www.automate.org/robotics/news/new-ansi-a3-r15-06-2025-american-national-standard-for-industrial-robot-safety-now-available-for-purchase)) — assume a **statically stable** machine: one that stays upright with the power cut. That's a fair assumption for a fixed-base industrial arm or a wheeled robot like a bricklaying machine. None of the three assume mud, slope, weather, or a site layout that changes weekly.

It's not a fair assumption for the robots actually being piloted on jobsites today: Boston Dynamics' Spot, Agility's Digit, and any humanoid. All of them are dynamically stable — they need continuous power and active balance control to stay upright, and they can fall if that control loop drops. The standard being built specifically for that failure mode, ISO/CD 25785-1 ("Robotics — Safety requirements for industrial mobile robots with actively controlled stability"), covers exactly this gap — legged, wheeled-balancing, and humanoid robots — but it's still at working-draft stage inside ISO TC299's working group, with a draft international standard expected sometime in 2026 and final publication realistically landing in late 2026 or 2027, not before ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/91469.html); [There's A Robot For That](https://theresarobotforthat.com/blog/humanoid-robot-safety-standards-2026/)). The parallel U.S./Canada effort, ANSI/A3 TR R15.108, is at an even earlier technical-report stage. Until either publishes, there is no accredited standard that actually matches the robots your site is most likely to see.

## Comparison: what covers a jobsite robot today, and what doesn't

| Framework | What it covers | Status (Sept 2026) | Does it cover legged/dynamically-stable robots (Spot, Digit, humanoids)? |
|---|---|---|---|
| IEC 61508 / ISO 13849 (via NVIDIA Halos) | Functional safety of the compute and control software | Active; Digit is the first robot in the Halos inspection lab | Partially — covers the software/electronics, not the fall/balance risk itself |
| ANSI/RIA R15.08 | Industrial mobile robots working near people, cage-free | Active; Digit passed a field evaluation against it in Nov. 2025 | Yes, but for structured indoor floors — not outdoor jobsite terrain |
| ANSI/A3 R15.06-2025 | US industrial robot & robot-cell safety | Published October 2025, in force | No — assumes statically stable machines |
| ISO 10218:2025 | International industrial robot safety | Published 2025, in force | No — same static-stability assumption |
| ISO/CD 25785-1 | Safety requirements for dynamically stable robots | Working draft at ISO TC299; DIS expected 2026, final publication 2026–2027 | Yes, by design — but unusable until it's finalized |
| ANSI/A3 TR R15.108 | US/Canada technical report for dynamically stable mobile robots incl. humanoids | Early-stage technical report in development | Yes, by design — earlier stage than ISO/CD 25785-1 |
| OSHA General Duty Clause | Catch-all for any recognized workplace hazard | Always in force; no robot-specific standard exists | Applies by default, with no robot-specific guidance |
| RCO (Robot Certified Operator) | Human operator competency, not the machine | Active, industry-run credential | N/A — certifies the person, not the platform |

## What governs liability on your site right now, then?

OSHA's General Duty Clause — Section 5(a)(1) of the OSH Act — the provision used any time a recognized workplace hazard has no standard of its own ([OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/section5-duties)). OSHA confirms there's still no robotics-specific standard; its own guidance points to the general machinery chapter of the OSHA Technical Manual and to industry standards like ANSI/A3 R15.06 as reference points, not requirements ([OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/robotics/standards)). To cite an employer under the General Duty Clause, OSHA has to show the hazard was recognized, likely to cause serious harm, and correctable with a feasible fix — which means your paper trail (vendor safety documentation, site risk assessment, incident history) does most of the work if something goes wrong. That's the same liability structure we lay out for AI decision-making generally in [our guide to AI risk and liability in construction](/seo/construction-ai-risk-liability-security-guide) — the vendor's contract caps their exposure; the GC's exposure is what's actually on the line.

## Does an operator certification cover the gap?

It covers a different gap. RCO (Robot Certified Operator) — developed with input from Tesla's Optimus program, Boston Dynamics field teams, and Fortune 500 automation directors — certifies that the person running the robot knows what they're doing, not that the machine itself is safe ([Robotomated](https://robotomated.com/certify)). Boston Dynamics' own training program points operators toward at least a Foundation-level credential before running Spot on a live site ([Boston Dynamics](https://bostondynamics.com/blog/introducing-the-latest-evolution-in-robotics-training-and-certification/)). Require it — but don't mistake it for a machine safety case. It's a floor, not the whole answer.

## Is anyone actually building a construction-specific safety case?

One credible effort: Built Robotics and the University of Pennsylvania's xLAB (Safe Autonomous Systems Lab) announced a research partnership in June to close exactly this gap — using real jobsite data from Built's autonomous survey and piling equipment (more than 50,000 operating hours across 40+ solar sites) to train and validate personnel-detection AI for unstructured, outdoor conditions ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/built-robotics-and-penn-engineerings-xlab-partner-to-advance-physical-ai-in-construction-302802050.html)). It's a research program, not a certification, and its first phase covers solar-site equipment specifically — but it's still the only initiative we're tracking that's explicitly building a safety case for construction terrain rather than adapting one built for a warehouse floor.

## What to ask before you sign a robot pilot agreement

Three questions, in order, before any robot — humanoid, quadruped, or otherwise — sets foot on your site:

1. **Which certification is this robot actually pursuing** — IEC 61508, ISO 13849, ANSI/RIA R15.08, both — and through which third-party certifier?
2. **What real-world safety data exists from outdoor, unstructured deployments** — not fulfillment-center or factory hours, which don't transfer to your terrain?
3. **Does the vendor require, or even offer, an operator certification** like RCO for your crew, separate from the machine's own paperwork?

A vendor who can answer all three with specifics is building a real safety case. One who can't is asking your GL policy to absorb the gap.
