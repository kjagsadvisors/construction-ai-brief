Illinois has regulated employer use of AI in hiring since January 1, 2026. Colorado's version doesn't take effect until January 1, 2027, after lawmakers gutted and delayed the original law weeks before it was due to start. If your company hires in either state — or uses the same national applicant tracking system across both — you're already on a compliance clock in one state and a countdown in the other. Here's exactly what each law covers, what's still unsettled, and what to have in place before your next hiring cycle.

## Does Illinois' AI hiring law apply to my company right now?

Yes, if you recruit, hire, promote, discipline, or discharge anyone in Illinois and any tool in that process uses algorithmic scoring or ranking. House Bill 3773 amended the Illinois Human Rights Act, and it has been in effect since January 1, 2026 ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/illinois-anti-discrimination-law-address-ai-goes-effect-1-january-2026)). It bars employers from using AI in a way that discriminates against a protected class in recruitment, hiring, promotion, training selection, discharge, discipline, or the "terms, privileges, or conditions of employment" — and it specifically calls out zip code as a discrimination proxy, since zip code can correlate with race and national origin in ways a generic screening algorithm won't flag on its own.

The law also requires employers to notify employees and applicants when AI is being used to influence one of those decisions. That notice duty is on the books now. What's still unresolved is the exact form the notice has to take.

## What does the Illinois notice requirement actually look like right now?

Less settled than it should be, six months in. The Illinois Department of Human Rights (IDHR) published draft notice regulations in mid-May 2026 and opened a public comment period, with a hearing scheduled for June 10. On June 2, IDHR withdrew the draft rules and postponed that hearing, saying it needed more time to coordinate with other state agencies, and it has not published a revised timeline since ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/illinois-department-of-human-rights-temporarily-withdraws-proposed-rules-on-use-of-artificial-intelligence-in-employment.html); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/illinois-postpones-proposed-regulations-ai-employment)).

That leaves employers in an awkward spot: the statutory nondiscrimination and notice obligations are enforceable today, but the state hasn't finalized what a compliant notice looks like. The practical move is to write a plain-language disclosure now — a sentence or two telling candidates that software contributes to screening and that they can ask about it — rather than waiting for a rule that keeps slipping.

## Is Colorado's AI hiring law in effect yet?

No, and it almost was under much stricter terms. The original Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) was scheduled to take effect June 30, 2026, and would have been one of the most comprehensive state AI employment laws in the country. A federal court paused enforcement of it in late April, and on May 14, Governor Polis signed SB 26-189, which repealed the original act outright and replaced it with a narrower "automated decision-making technology" (ADMT) framework that doesn't start until January 1, 2027 ([Colorado General Assembly, SB26-189](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/colorado-governor-signs-sb-189)).

## What will the Colorado law actually require once it starts?

Three concrete obligations for any employer using a "covered ADMT" — a tool that materially influences hiring, promotion, termination, or compensation decisions:

1. **Notice before use.** Employers must give candidates and employees clear notice before deploying a covered ADMT in a decision affecting them.
2. **An adverse-action process.** Within 30 days of an adverse outcome — a rejection, a denied promotion — the employer must give the individual a plain-language explanation of the ADMT's role and a path to request human review ([Littler](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/colorado-amends-its-artificial-intelligence-law-substantially-reducing)).
3. **Three-year record retention.** Employers and the software developers they use must retain compliance records for at least three years.

Enforcement sits solely with the Colorado Attorney General — the law does not create a private right of action, so an individual applicant can't bring their own AI-hiring claim under the state statute.

## Which of our hiring tools are actually covered?

More than most construction HR teams assume. If your applicant tracking system uses Indeed's or LinkedIn's built-in candidate ranking, or Workday's screening module scores resumes, that's algorithmic influence on a hiring decision — the trigger for both laws, regardless of whether the vendor markets it as "AI." Craft-labor recruiting (electricians, pipefitters, ironworkers, supers) is exactly where high-volume, time-pressured screening tools tend to live, which is exactly where the regulatory exposure accumulates.

Colorado's law does carve specific administrative tools out of the ADMT definition entirely — identity verification, fraud-monitoring, and "routine calendar scheduling and meeting coordination software" are excluded. But Colorado's list of covered employment decisions itself includes scheduling where an algorithm materially affects hours or compensation, so don't assume a tool that touches shift assignment is automatically exempt just because it's schedule-related — that distinction is narrow enough to warrant a specific question to the vendor, not an assumption.

## Illinois vs. Colorado: what's actually required, side by side

| | Illinois (HB 3773) | Colorado (SB 26-189) |
|---|---|---|
| Status | In effect since January 1, 2026 | Not in effect until January 1, 2027 |
| Covers | Recruitment, hiring, promotion, training selection, discharge, discipline, tenure | Hiring, termination, promotion, compensation (as "consequential decisions") |
| Notice trigger | AI used to influence a covered decision | Covered ADMT materially influences a consequential decision |
| Adverse-action duty | Ongoing nondiscrimination obligation; no fixed response window in statute | Plain-language explanation + human-review path within 30 days |
| Zip code as proxy | Explicitly barred | Not separately addressed |
| Record retention | Not specified in statute | At least 3 years (developer and employer) |
| Who can enforce | IDHR / Illinois Human Rights Commission — actual damages, civil penalties, attorney's fees | Colorado Attorney General only — no private right of action |
| Implementing rules | Draft notice rules withdrawn June 2, 2026; no revised timeline | Rulemaking not yet underway ahead of 2027 |

## What should we actually do this quarter?

Three things, regardless of which state you hire in:

- **Run the vendor inventory.** List every tool your HR team uses for recruiting, screening, or evaluation, and ask each vendor in writing whether it uses algorithmic scoring or ranking and which decisions it touches. Most contractors have never asked this question of an ATS or job-board vendor.
- **Draft the disclosure once, use it everywhere.** A two-sentence notice — software contributes to this decision, here's how to ask about it — covers Illinois now and gets you ahead of Colorado's 2027 start date. Write it once and apply it across both states rather than drafting state-specific language.
- **Assign an owner for adverse-action questions.** If an algorithmic screen filters out a candidate for a journeyman electrician posting, someone needs to own the documentation and field the candidate's question about it. Colorado will require a response within 30 days once its law starts; building that process now costs less than building it under a deadline.

Neither state is running an aggressive enforcement program today — Illinois' rules are unsettled, and Colorado's AG has said enforcement won't begin until rulemaking on the new framework concludes. That's not the same as no exposure: Illinois' underlying nondiscrimination duty has applied for six months regardless of rulemaking status, and a company with zero disclosure process today has a harder time arguing good-faith compliance if an actual complaint arrives later.
