Illinois has regulated employer use of AI in hiring since January 1, 2026, though the state withdrew its draft notice rules in June and hasn't replaced them. Colorado's version takes effect January 1, 2027, and the Attorney General's revised draft rules, published October 6, 2026, presume that any candidate score or ranking a hiring manager sees is covered. If your company hires in either state — or uses the same national applicant tracking system across both — you're already on a compliance clock in one state and about seven weeks from the start date in the other. Here's exactly what each law covers, what's newly settled, and what to have in place before your next hiring cycle.

## Does Illinois' AI hiring law apply to my company right now?

Yes, if you recruit, hire, promote, discipline, or discharge anyone in Illinois and any tool in that process uses algorithmic scoring or ranking. House Bill 3773 amended the Illinois Human Rights Act, and it has been in effect since January 1, 2026 ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/illinois-anti-discrimination-law-address-ai-goes-effect-1-january-2026)). It bars employers from using AI in a way that discriminates against a protected class in recruitment, hiring, promotion, training selection, discharge, discipline, or the "terms, privileges, or conditions of employment" — and it specifically calls out zip code as a discrimination proxy, since zip code can correlate with race and national origin in ways a generic screening algorithm won't flag on its own.

The law also requires employers to notify employees and applicants when AI is being used to influence one of those decisions. That notice duty is on the books now. What's still unresolved, more than ten months in, is the exact form the notice has to take.

## What does the Illinois notice requirement actually look like right now?

Still unsettled. The Illinois Department of Human Rights (IDHR) published draft notice regulations on May 15, 2026 and opened a public comment period, with a hearing scheduled for June 10. On June 2, IDHR withdrew the draft rules and cancelled that hearing, saying it needed more time to coordinate with other state agencies ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/illinois-department-of-human-rights-temporarily-withdraws-proposed-rules-on-use-of-artificial-intelligence-in-employment.html); [DataGuidance](https://www.dataguidance.com/news/illinois-idhr-postpones-hearing-and-temporarily)). Two independent trackers updated on September 25, 2026 reported that the agency had not refiled and that no replacement rules had been adopted ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/guides/illinois-hb-3773-ai-employment-discrimination); [InterviewerSignal](https://interviewersignal.com/blog/illinois-hb-3773-ai-in-employment)). When we rechecked in mid-November 2026, we found no report of a new proposal.

That leaves employers in the same awkward spot they were in this spring: the statutory nondiscrimination and notice obligations are enforceable today, but the state hasn't finalized what a compliant notice looks like. The practical move is still to write a plain-language disclosure now — a sentence or two telling candidates that software contributes to screening and that they can ask about it — rather than waiting for a rule that keeps slipping.

## Is Colorado's AI hiring law in effect yet?

No, but the compliance picture got a lot more concrete this fall. The original Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) was scheduled to take effect June 30, 2026, and would have been one of the most comprehensive state AI employment laws in the country. A federal court paused enforcement of it in late April, and on May 14, Governor Polis signed SB 26-189, which repealed the original act outright and replaced it with a narrower Automated Decision-Making Technology (ADMT) framework that starts January 1, 2027 ([Colorado General Assembly, SB26-189](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/colorado-governor-signs-sb-189)).

Unlike the original law, rulemaking under SB 26-189 is mandatory rather than optional, and it has to be finished before the law takes effect. The Colorado Attorney General's office collected informal comments through mid-July, then on August 11, 2026 published formal draft rules for the ADMT Act alongside the state's new Chatbot Safety Act. ([Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-Colorado-Proposes-Detailed-Rules-Implementing-New-ADMT-and-Chatbot-Laws); [Consumer Finance Monitor](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2026/08/12/colorado-publishes-draft-admt-regulations/)).

After reviewing more than 60 stakeholder comments filed by September 4, the Department of Law released a revised interim draft on October 6, 2026 ([Colorado Department of Law redline](https://coag.gov/app/uploads/2026/10/2026.10.06-ADMT-Chatbot-Act-Rulemaking-Interim-Draft-Redline.pdf); [Moore & Van Allen](https://www.mvalaw.com/data-points/colorados-interim-draft-admt-rules-what-businesses-need-to-know-now)). The rulemaking hearing and the written-comment deadline both fell on October 26. The statute requires the Attorney General to adopt final rules by January 1, 2027, the same day the law takes effect. As of mid-November 2026 we found no public report that the final text had been adopted, so treat the October draft as the best available guide, not the last word.

## What will the Colorado law actually require once it starts?

The statute sets three concrete obligations for any employer using a "covered ADMT" — a tool that materially influences hiring, promotion, termination, or compensation decisions — and the draft rules now spell out what compliance with each one looks like in practice:

1. **Notice before use.** Employers must give candidates and employees clear notice before deploying a covered ADMT in a decision affecting them.
2. **An adverse-action process.** Within 30 days of an adverse outcome — a rejection, a denied promotion — the employer must give the individual a plain-language explanation of the ADMT's role and a path to request human review ([Littler](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/colorado-amends-its-artificial-intelligence-law-substantially-reducing)).
3. **Three-year record retention.** Employers and the software developers they use must retain compliance records for at least three years.

The draft rules fill in what the statute left open, and they create more operational work than the statute suggests ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/colorado-releases-proposed-rules-for-its-ai-and-chatbot-safety-laws-these-create-more-operational-work-than-the-statutes-suggestlegalupdate.html)). Enforcement sits solely with the Colorado Attorney General. The law does not create a private right of action, so an individual applicant can't bring their own AI-hiring claim under the state statute. Until January 1, 2030, the Attorney General must generally give 60 days' notice and a chance to cure before bringing an action, where a cure is possible ([Secure Privacy](https://secureprivacy.ai/blog/colorado-admt-act-2026-guide)).

## What changed in Colorado's October 2026 revised draft rules?

The biggest change is a single test for "materially influence," and it's a broad one. The August draft floated two competing standards and asked for comment. The October 6 revision replaced them with a rebuttable presumption ([Ballard Spahr](https://www.cyberadviserblog.com/2026/10/revised-draft-rules-for-the-colorado-admt-act-and-conversational-ai-act-released/); [Stauss Firm](https://staussfirm.com/2026/10/13/colorado-attorney-general-publishes-updated-admt-act-draft-rules/)).

Under the revised Rule 6, an output is presumed to materially influence a decision if it constrains an option set, sets a threshold, or produces a rank, score, classification, recommendation, or prediction about a specific person, and if the decision-maker reviews it (or it screens what the decision-maker sees) and the outcome is consistent with it. In hiring terms: if your ATS scores an electrician's application at 42 out of 100, your recruiter sees that score, and the applicant is rejected, the draft presumes the score mattered.

The employer can rebut the presumption by showing the output was a "de minimis factor," defined as one that is not a substantial factor in the decision. The draft lists the kind of evidence that counts ([Moore & Van Allen](https://www.mvalaw.com/data-points/colorados-interim-draft-admt-rules-what-businesses-need-to-know-now)):

- whether the decision-maker independently reviewed substantial primary evidence, such as the actual resume and certifications
- whether a consistently applied cut-off or policy rule, not the model output, drove the outcome
- whether the volume of data was more than a person could reasonably review in the time available

That last point is the one high-volume craft recruiting should worry about. A recruiter clearing 300 applications for 12 openings in an afternoon is not independently reviewing each one, and the draft treats that as evidence the software made the call.

The same draft sets the response clocks an HR team has to be able to hit ([Moore & Van Allen](https://www.mvalaw.com/data-points/colorados-interim-draft-admt-rules-what-businesses-need-to-know-now)):

| Obligation under the October 2026 draft | Deadline |
|---|---|
| Adverse-outcome disclosure to the candidate or employee | 30 days after the decision |
| Response to a request for more information about the ADMT | 15 days |
| Acknowledge a request for meaningful human review | 10 days |
| Complete the human review and respond | 45 days |

The disclosure itself has to identify the decision, describe the tool's role accurately, say what human reviewers were involved, and explain how to ask for more. The October revision dropped the August draft's standalone requirement to list the "principal reasons" for the outcome, which makes the notice easier to template.

One more point for contractors: you stay responsible for your deployer obligations even when a staffing agency or outsourced recruiter runs the tool for you. If a third party screens your craft hires, the contract needs to say who sends the notice and who does the human review.

## Which of our hiring tools are actually covered?

More than most construction HR teams assume. If your applicant tracking system uses Indeed's or LinkedIn's built-in candidate ranking, or Workday's screening module scores resumes, that's algorithmic influence on a hiring decision — the trigger for both laws, regardless of whether the vendor markets it as "AI." Craft-labor recruiting (electricians, pipefitters, ironworkers, supers) is exactly where high-volume, time-pressured screening tools tend to live, which is exactly where the regulatory exposure accumulates.

Colorado's law does carve specific administrative tools out of the ADMT definition entirely — identity verification, fraud-monitoring, and "routine calendar scheduling and meeting coordination software" are excluded. But Colorado's list of covered employment decisions itself includes scheduling where an algorithm materially affects hours or compensation, so don't assume a tool that touches shift assignment is automatically exempt just because it's schedule-related — that distinction is narrow enough to warrant a specific question to the vendor, not an assumption.

## Illinois vs. Colorado: what's actually required, side by side

| | Illinois (HB 3773) | Colorado (SB 26-189) |
|---|---|---|
| Status | In effect since January 1, 2026 | Not in effect until January 1, 2027 |
| Covers | Recruitment, hiring, promotion, training selection, discharge, discipline, tenure | Hiring, termination, promotion, compensation (as "consequential decisions") |
| Notice trigger | AI used to influence a covered decision | Covered ADMT materially influences a consequential decision |
| Adverse-action duty | Ongoing nondiscrimination obligation; no fixed response window in statute | Plain-language explanation + human-review path within 30 days; draft rules add 10 days to acknowledge a review request and 45 to complete it |
| Zip code as proxy | Explicitly barred | Not separately addressed |
| Record retention | Not specified in statute | At least 3 years (developer and employer) |
| Who can enforce | IDHR / Illinois Human Rights Commission — actual damages, civil penalties, attorney's fees | Colorado Attorney General only — no private right of action; 60 days' notice and chance to cure, where possible, until January 1, 2030 |
| Implementing rules | Draft notice rules published May 15, 2026 and withdrawn June 2; no refiling reported as of late September | Draft published August 11, 2026; revised October 6; hearing held October 26; final rules due by January 1, 2027 |

## What should we actually do this quarter?

Four things, regardless of which state you hire in:

- **Run the vendor inventory.** List every tool your HR team uses for recruiting, screening, or evaluation, and ask each vendor in writing whether it uses algorithmic scoring or ranking and which decisions it touches. Most contractors have never asked this question of an ATS or job-board vendor.
- **Draft the disclosure once, use it everywhere.** A two-sentence notice — software contributes to this decision, here's how to ask about it — covers Illinois now and gets you ahead of Colorado's 2027 start date. 
- **Name the human reviewer and test the clock.** If you hire in Colorado, pick the person who will handle review requests, confirm they have authority to overturn a rejection, and walk one fake request through the 10-day acknowledgment and 45-day completion deadlines before January 1. A process that exists only in a policy document won't survive its first real request.
- **Build Illinois on Colorado's draft.** Illinois has given employers nothing comparable, so use the Colorado draft as the template for both states and adjust when the final text is published.

What isn't worth doing yet: paying for a third-party bias audit of a tool you haven't even inventoried, or buying a compliance platform before Colorado's rules are final. The inventory and the notice cost a few hours and cover most of the exposure a 50-person sub actually has.

Neither state is running an aggressive enforcement program today. Illinois' rules are still unsettled more than ten months after the law took effect, and Colorado gives employers a 60-day cure window through 2029. That's not the same as no exposure: Illinois' underlying nondiscrimination duty has applied since January regardless of rulemaking status, and a company with zero disclosure process today has a harder time arguing good-faith compliance if an actual complaint arrives later.
