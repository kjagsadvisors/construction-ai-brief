AI submittal review has been marketed as a spec-comparison problem — feed it a cut sheet, feed it a spec section, get a compliance flag back. In practice it's an extraction problem first. Most submittal packages aren't clean, uniform PDFs: they're scanned faxes, phone-photographed shop drawings, and dense equipment tables buried inside a 40-page manufacturer catalog excerpt. Before any AI can compare a submittal against a spec, it has to correctly pull structured data — the right table, the right field, the right dimension — out of a document that wasn't built to be machine-read. That step is where most of the failures actually happen. The industry started seriously fixing it in mid-2026, and by September the fixes have moved from standalone tools into the platforms GCs already use.

## What actually goes wrong when AI tries to read a submittal PDF?

Two different things fail, and they fail for different reasons.

The first is raw text extraction on a bad scan: fax artifacts, skewed pages, a stamp overlapping the product table, a fifth-generation photocopy of a cut sheet. Standard OCR degrades here the same way it always has — wrong characters, missing rows, a table that reads as a paragraph.

The second, more common failure isn't about legibility at all. It's about structure. A cut sheet's product table might scan perfectly and still get misread if the extraction tool doesn't know that column three is voltage and column five is a footnote, not a value. That's the difference between OCR — turning pixels into text — and structured extraction, which returns labeled fields, table cells, and document sections an AI can actually reason over. A submittal comparison built on unstructured text output will misfire even on a document that scanned cleanly.

## Why do construction drawings extract so much worse than spec text?

Because they're a different kind of document, not just a harder version of the same one. Specs, product data sheets, and O&M submittals are text-heavy — paragraphs, tables, numbered requirements — and current extraction models handle that format well. Drawings are visual and spatial: dimension strings, revision clouds, symbol layers, notes that only make sense relative to where they sit on the sheet. Most AI tools embedded in construction platforms, including the agent suites Procore and others shipped through 2026, process text-based documents reasonably well and treat drawings as a separate, harder problem.

Trunk Tools built its Cortex platform specifically to close that gap, and the company says it took four years of training bespoke models on 2D construction drawings to get there — a specific enough claim about the difficulty that it's worth taking at face value. At launch, Trunk Tools reported Cortex reviewing a 20-sheet change bulletin, including changes the architect didn't cloud, in under five minutes ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/06/17/3313698/0/en/trunk-tools-launches-cortex-to-tackle-construction-s-hardest-ai-problem-drawings.html); [our full breakdown of Cortex's launch](/posts/2026-06-21-trunk-tools-cortex-construction-drawings-ai)). That's a text-and-drawing-reading problem, not a pure OCR problem — which is exactly why it took years longer to ship than text-only submittal review did.

## What's actually shipping to fix the extraction layer?

The clearest development is on the text side. Mistral released OCR 4 in June, a document intelligence model built for structured extraction rather than plain OCR: it returns text blocks with page coordinates, labels each block by type (table, header, footnote, signature), and attaches confidence scores at the word and block level. It processes PDF, DOC, PPT, and OpenDocument formats, and — notably for a sub whose submittal packages carry pricing or manufacturer terms — it deploys as a self-hosted Docker container instead of requiring documents to leave your network ([Mistral](https://mistral.ai/news/ocr-4/)). Independent benchmark testing (OlmOCRBench) put it ahead of every competing system tested in the majority of head-to-head comparisons, though that number comes from Mistral's own release materials, not a third-party construction-document evaluation ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/06/23/mistral-ocr-4/); [Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/318978/20260624/mistral-ocr-4-ships-structure-aware-document-ai-runs-your-own-infrastructure.htm); [our workflow breakdown of OCR 4 for submittals](/posts/2026-06-28-mistral-ocr-4-submittal-extraction-construction)). Mistral followed up with OCR 4.1 in July, adding native paragraph-level bounding boxes and block-level confidence scores on top of the existing word- and page-level output — a smaller upgrade, but a useful one for anyone building a comparison layer that needs to know exactly how much to trust a given table cell before flagging it ([Mistral Docs](https://docs.mistral.ai/models/ocr-4-1)).

On the platform side, the bigger change since June is distribution. TrunkSubmittal now runs as a workflow step directly inside Procore and Autodesk Forma — it starts working the moment a submittal enters either platform, with no separate login or manual routing — and Trunk Tools says the agent is 71% faster than it was at its original release ([Trunk Tools](https://trunktools.com/trunksubmittal/)). That matters more for adoption than for the underlying extraction science: a GC coordinator who never has to leave Procore is a lot more likely to actually use the tool than one who has to hop into a third system.

The realistic pipeline this enables: submittal PDF and spec section both go through structured extraction first, then a separate comparison model — a GC platform's agent, or a purpose-built tool — reasons over the structured output to flag mismatches. Extraction and comparison are still two distinct jobs. Fixing the first doesn't automatically fix the second.

## Extraction approaches compared

| Approach | What it handles well | Where it still breaks | Rough cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mistral OCR 4.1 (self-hosted or API)** | Text-heavy documents: spec sections, product data sheets, O&M submittals; structured table and field output with block-level confidence | Dense graphical drawings with heavy spatial reliance — architectural sheets, structural details, MEP symbol layers | $2 per 1,000 pages (batch) to $5 per 1,000 pages (Document AI); self-hostable at no per-page fee beyond compute |
| **Trunk Tools Cortex / TrunkSubmittal** | Both text and 2D drawings, purpose-built for construction document conventions; now embedded directly in Procore and Autodesk Forma as a workflow step | Judgment calls on ambiguous spec language or substitution requests | Subscription, layered on top of Procore or Autodesk Construction Cloud |
| **Native GC platform OCR (baseline agents)** | Standard-format PDFs already organized inside the platform | Older scans, non-standard formats, anything outside the platform's document pipeline | Usually bundled into existing platform contract |
| **Manual review (status quo)** | Everything — a person can read anything eventually | Volume; a 200–400 item submittal log at two hours per review doesn't scale | Coordinator/PE labor hours |

## Is fixing extraction actually worth it, or is this a solved problem already?

For a GC or MEP sub whose submittal log clears 150+ items on a job, yes — extraction quality is the ceiling on how much of the review process AI can touch at all. Gilbane's rollout of Trunk Tools' submittal agent flagged as many as 72% of first-pass submittals as non-compliant, with only 3% fully compliant on the first read, and reported close to a 50% cut in re-submission cycle time once that first pass ran reliably ([ENR](https://www.enr.com/articles/61435-gilbane-rolls-out-trunk-tools-ai-agents-across-its-jobsites)). Trunk Tools' own tracking, published alongside the Cortex launch, puts the median TrunkSubmittal user's cycle time down 74% over the trailing 12 months (54.8 days to 14.0 days) and the share of submittals stuck past 60 days for approval down from roughly 43% to 2% ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/06/17/3313698/0/en/trunk-tools-launches-cortex-to-tackle-construction-s-hardest-ai-problem-drawings.html)) — a vendor number, not an independent audit, but directionally consistent with the Gilbane figures and with why a broken extraction layer is expensive at volume, not just annoying. One vendor's own accounting puts the average cost of a rejected submittal at roughly $805 in administrative time plus 2 to 4 weeks of added cycle time ([BuildSync](https://buildsync.ai/resources/the-true-cost-of-a-rejected-submittal-805-each)).

If your log runs low volume and a coordinator with a clean template already keeps pace, the extraction problem isn't costing you enough yet to chase. The test that actually tells you something: run one Division 22 or 23 submittal package that includes at least one scanned or hand-marked page through whichever extraction tool your GC or vendor offers, and check whether the output correctly separates the product table from the footnotes before you trust it with a spec comparison. If it can't do that cleanly, no downstream AI review is going to be reliable either.
