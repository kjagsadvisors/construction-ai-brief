Yes, to a degree that's already measurable in 2026 — not in the form of your submittal tool going dark, but in slower responses, rate limits, and occasional outages during peak hours. The AI labs that construction software vendors build on are themselves capacity-constrained: Google has been rationing its own Gemini models for Meta since March, and Anthropic is paying $1.25 billion a month to rent a dedicated data center because it can't build fast enough to keep up with demand. Every AI feature riding on that infrastructure — submittal review, RFI drafting, takeoff assistance — inherits the same constraint, one layer removed.

## Is the compute shortage actually real, or is this a vendor excuse?

It's real, and it's coming from the AI labs themselves, not from a construction software vendor covering for a bad release. Google confirmed to the *Financial Times* that it has restricted Meta's access to its Gemini models since March 2026 because it can't supply enough capacity to meet demand — this for one of its largest customers, while Google itself is spending more than $180 billion this year on infrastructure ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/28/google-limits-metas-use-of-its-gemini-ai-models-ft-reports.html)). Google is meanwhile paying SpaceX $920 million a month for emergency access to roughly 110,000 Nvidia GPUs at a Memphis data center, what it calls "bridge capacity" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/05/google-will-pay-spacex-920m-per-month-for-compute/)).

Anthropic's version of the same story is larger. After revenue grew 80-fold in a single quarter — CEO Dario Amodei called the pace "too hard to handle" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/06/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-says-company-crew-80-fold-in-first-quarter.html)) — Anthropic signed a deal to rent the entire AI capacity of SpaceX's Colossus 1 data center: more than 220,000 Nvidia GPUs and 300 megawatts of power, for roughly $1.25 billion a month through 2029 ([Data Center Dynamics](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/spacex-ipo-filing-reveals-anthropic-set-to-pay-musks-firm-125bn-a-month-to-rent-xai-data-center-space/); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/anthropic-to-rent-all-ai-capacity-at-spacexs-colossus-data-center-180327774.html)). OpenAI's CFO Sarah Friar has said the same thing in plainer terms: "We're making some very tough trades at the moment and things we're not pursuing because we don't have enough compute" ([AOL/Fortune](https://www.aol.com/articles/openais-cfo-says-company-passing-112355988.html)). None of these three companies benefits from inventing a compute shortage — it's a constraint they're spending billions trying to escape.

## How would a shortage at Google or Anthropic actually show up in your project software?

Indirectly, but concretely. Submittal-review copilots, RFI drafting assistants, scheduling agents, and vision-based safety monitoring tools don't run their own models — they call inference capacity purchased from Google Cloud, AWS, Azure, or a frontier lab's API, the same pool Google and Anthropic are rationing among their biggest direct customers. A construction software vendor is one account among thousands bidding for that same capacity. It has no structural reason to get priority over Meta or a Fortune 500 enterprise customer, and every reason to get throttled first when the provider hits a ceiling.

That shows up three ways: the feature runs noticeably slower during your region's peak hours, requests get rate-limited or queued, or the vendor quietly routes traffic to a cheaper, less capable model to keep the lights on without telling you accuracy dropped. Anthropic's own outage pattern is the clearest public evidence of the first two — by mid-June 2026, Claude had logged its tenth significant service disruption in twelve days, and Anthropic's status updates pointed to demand growing faster than infrastructure, "particularly at peak hours" ([Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/318514/20260616/claude-outage-tenth-disruption-12-days-exposes-anthropic-infrastructure-strain.htm)). By late June, outage-tracking sites had logged more than 8,000 user reports of elevated errors in a single stretch ([Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/318925/20260623/claude-outage-tops-8000-reports-agentic-pipeline-failures-mount-before-anthropic-ipo.htm)). The third failure mode — silent model downgrade — is harder to prove from the outside, which is exactly why it's worth asking your vendor about directly rather than assuming it isn't happening.

## Who's rationing compute right now, and what are they paying to fix it?

| Company | Evidence of constraint | What they're doing about it |
|---|---|---|
| Google | Rationing Gemini access for Meta since March 2026, despite $180B+ in 2026 capex | Paying SpaceX $920M/month for ~110,000 GPUs of "bridge capacity" |
| Anthropic | 80-fold quarterly revenue growth outpaced planned infrastructure; 10 Claude outages in 12 days (mid-June) | Renting all of SpaceX's Colossus 1 data center — 220,000+ GPUs, $1.25B/month through 2029 |
| OpenAI | CFO says the company is "passing on opportunities" and making "tough trades" due to compute limits | Continued hyperscaler capacity deals and its own inference-chip development |
| Meta | AI token usage restricted by its own primary model supplier (Google) | Reassigned ~7,000 employees to AI divisions; leaning on its own Muse Spark model |

The pattern across all four: the shortage is structural, not a temporary blip one vendor is managing badly. Every company on this list is a buyer with essentially unlimited budget for compute, and none of them can fully buy their way out of it yet.

## What should you actually ask your AI vendor about this?

Three questions, and a vendor's ability to answer them tells you more than any feature demo:

1. **What happens to this feature under load?** A rate limit, a queue, or a silent fallback to a smaller model — ask directly which one, and get it in writing if the answer matters to your workflow.
2. **Does your SLA actually cover the AI feature, or just the core platform?** Most software SLAs guarantee uptime for the base application. Very few extend that guarantee to the AI layer specifically, and the gap is usually in the fine print, not the sales deck.
3. **Do you have a reserved-capacity arrangement with your model provider, or are you buying on the open market?** Vendors serious about AI reliability have already asked their own suppliers this question. If your rep can't answer it, they haven't asked.

## Is this going to get better or worse before it gets better?

Worse before better, on the industry's own timeline. New data center capacity takes one to two years to come online from the point it's financed, so the buildout announced in 2026 doesn't relieve pressure until 2027 or later at the earliest ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-shortage-2026/)). Anthropic's Colossus deal runs through 2029. That means the capacity constraint behind today's rate limits and outages is a multi-year condition to plan around, not a one-quarter glitch to wait out.

## What this means for how you use AI in your workflow today

None of this is a reason to hold off on AI features that are already saving real time on submittal prep, RFI drafting, or takeoff — the productivity case for those tools doesn't disappear because the infrastructure underneath them is tight. It's a reason to stop treating an AI feature as guaranteed-uptime infrastructure the way you'd treat email or your document repository. Keep anything genuinely time-sensitive — a bid-day deadline, a same-day RFI response with contractual consequences — on a workflow with a human fallback, and reserve the AI feature for the volume of work where a slow afternoon costs you convenience, not a missed deadline.
