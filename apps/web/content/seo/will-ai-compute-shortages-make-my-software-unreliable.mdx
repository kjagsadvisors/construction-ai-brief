Yes. Plan on the AI features in your project software being less reliable than the software around them, for at least the next two years. Claude went down twice on November 13, 2026. A Microsoft 365 outage the same week broke the Outlook connectors in both Claude and ChatGPT. And the power-equipment backlog behind the compute shortage now runs into 2027 and beyond. Every submittal-review, RFI-drafting, or takeoff feature your construction software vendor built on those services inherits that fragility, one layer removed.

## Is the compute shortage actually real, or is this a vendor excuse?

It's real, and the strongest evidence now comes from a securities filing, not a status page. Anthropic's draft IPO filing, as reported in October 2026, shows about $450 billion in "massive, noncancelable" cloud commitments over the next decade, mostly with Amazon and Google. It also says the company sought more capacity than the hyperscalers would supply ([24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/10/21/anthropic-has-committed-450-billion-to-cloud-computing-its-draft-ipo-filing-shows/)). That sits on top of the May deal to rent all of SpaceX's Colossus 1 data center, more than 220,000 Nvidia GPUs and 300 megawatts ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/06/anthropic-spacex-data-center-capacity.html); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/higher-limits-spacex)).

All of that spending still hasn't stopped the outages. Claude logged its 164th documented disruption of the year on August 5 ([Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/323171/20260805/claude-goes-down-again-71b-compute-deal-cannot-prevent-anthropics-164th-outage.htm)). On September 3, ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok all went down in the same window ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/03/chatgpt-claude-grok-outages); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/09/03/chatgpt-claude-and-grok-all-had-outages-at-the-same-time/5294322); [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/artificial-intelligence/anthropic-confirms-claude-is-down-multiple-models-affected/)). Users that fall saw the message "Due to unexpected capacity constraints, Claude is unable to respond to your message" ([StatusGator](https://statusgator.com/services/claude/outage-history)).

## What's actually been breaking since September?

The failures have kept coming, but they've changed shape. The outages are less about raw model capacity now and more about everything wrapped around it: sign-in, connectors, agents.

| Date (2026) | Service | What broke | Stated cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sept 29 | Claude | Errors across Claude, Claude Code, Cowork, and the API for about 36 minutes; single sign-on and file uploads failed after that ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/09/29/claude-confirmed-outage-sept-29/)) | None given |
| Nov 11 | ChatGPT | New Workspace Agent runs blocked for about 9 hours 45 minutes, flagged as major ([StatusGator](https://statusgator.com/services/openai/outage-history)) | Not stated |
| Nov 12–13 | Claude and ChatGPT | Microsoft 365 connector and Claude for Outlook down for about 24 hours; OpenAI's Microsoft 365 connector also hit ([AppleScoop](https://applescoop.org/story/every-major-outage-november-2026-timeline-faa-microsoft-chase-united); [StatusGator](https://statusgator.com/services/openai/outage-history)) | Microsoft Exchange Online gateway bug, not AI capacity |
| Nov 13 | Claude | Sign-in failure 09:58–11:58 UTC, then a broader claude.ai, API, and Claude Code outage 13:09–15:38 UTC; user reports peaked at 6,404 an hour ([Instatus](https://instatus.com/now/anthropic.com/outages/2026-11-13-09); [DEV](https://dev.to/its-tahir/claude-went-down-twice-on-13-november-what-broke-what-kept-working-and-what-to-do-next-time-5gll)) | None published |
| Nov 18 | Claude | Google Docs connector editing failed; reading still worked ([UptimeRobot](https://uptimerobot.com/is-it-down/anthropic/2026-11-18/)) | None published |

Two details from November 13 matter for anyone running AI workflows. During the first outage the Claude API stayed up, so teams with a backup API key could keep working. The second outage took the API down too, and scheduled tasks ran more than two hours late or were skipped ([DEV](https://dev.to/its-tahir/claude-went-down-twice-on-13-november-what-broke-what-kept-working-and-what-to-do-next-time-5gll)). If your vendor runs overnight batch jobs on one of these providers, like nightly submittal-log updates or morning RFI summaries, a skipped run can go unnoticed until someone asks where the report went.

## Are all of these outages caused by the compute shortage?

No. Our earlier version of this page blurred that, so here's the straight version. September's "capacity constraints" errors were explicitly about compute. Most November incidents came with no published cause. The November 12–13 connector failure traced to a bug in Microsoft's Exchange Online gateway servers, made worse by extra load and capacity that was already offline ([AppleScoop](https://applescoop.org/story/every-major-outage-november-2026-timeline-faa-microsoft-chase-united)).

For a contractor, the cause matters less than the pattern. An AI feature in your software depends on a longer chain than the software itself: the model provider, its compute supply, its sign-in system, and the Outlook or SharePoint connector it uses to read your email. Any link in that chain can break your RFI assistant while Procore or your document system stays up.

## Does this affect construction-specific software, or just chatbots?

It reaches construction tools too. Submittal copilots, RFI drafting assistants, scheduling agents, and estimating tools mostly don't run their own models. They call capacity from a frontier lab's API or from Google Cloud, AWS, or Azure. A construction software vendor is one account among thousands, with no structural reason to get priority over a lab's largest direct customers.

That shows up three ways. The feature slows down at peak hours. Requests get rate-limited or queued. Or the vendor quietly routes traffic to a cheaper, less capable model without telling you accuracy dropped. The first two are documented at the provider level. The third is hard to prove from the outside, which is why you should ask about it directly. For a worked example of a vendor dependency going wrong, see our coverage of [OpenAI shutting off its Sora API the day before a Claude outage](/posts/2026-09-30-sora-api-shutdown-claude-outage-construction-ai-vendor-dependency).

## When will the compute shortage ease?

Not on a software timeline. The binding constraint is now power equipment and grid connections, not chips. Generator step-up transformer lead times passed 160 weeks in the first quarter of 2026, up from a 143-week average in 2024, according to Wood Mackenzie figures reported by Reuters ([Wealthion](https://www.wealthion.com/learn/the-transformer-shortage-what-it-means-for-the-ai-buildout/)). PwC analysts put lead times for high-capacity units at up to four years ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/05/11/u-s-transformer-market-faces-severe-supply-constraints-as-lead-times-extend-to-four-years/)). Anthropic's own filing warns that power delays could slow the AI buildout, and that it "may bear these costs even if the underlying capacity is delayed or never realized" ([Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/10/27/2026/anthropic-says-energy-difficulties-could-slow-down-the-ai-buildout)).

How much of the pipeline is actually slipping is disputed. Sightline Climate estimated in February that 30–50% of the roughly 16 GW of U.S. data center projects planned for 2026 would be delayed to 2027 or later, or never built ([E&E News](https://www.eenews.net/articles/data-center-construction-delays-grow-report/)). SemiAnalysis argues that figure overstates the problem because the slippage is concentrated in early-stage announced projects, while its hyperscaler build forecasts moved only a few percent ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/stop-saying-half-of-2026-us-datacenter)). Both camps agree that real projects are slipping and that the bottleneck is power. Neither expects it to clear in 2027. For the construction side of that backlog, see our [guide to the data center construction boom](/seo/data-center-construction-boom-contractors-guide) and our coverage of [Anthropic's filing on power delays and grid-upgrade costs](/posts/2026-10-29-anthropic-s1-power-delays-grid-upgrade-costs-construction-electrical-sitework-subs-utility-delay-clauses).

## What should you actually ask your AI vendor?

Four questions. How a vendor answers them tells you more than any feature demo:

1. **What happens to this feature under load?** A rate limit, a queue, or a silent switch to a smaller model? Ask which one, and get it in writing if your workflow depends on the answer.
2. **Does your SLA cover the AI feature, or just the core platform?** Most SLAs guarantee uptime for the base application. Few extend it to the AI layer, and the gap is usually in the fine print.
3. **Do you have a second model provider you can fail over to?** November 13 showed that a backup API path kept some teams working while the consumer app was down. A vendor that's tied to one provider with no fallback goes down whenever that provider does.
4. **Which third-party connectors does the feature depend on?** If your RFI assistant reads from Outlook or SharePoint, a Microsoft outage takes it down even when the AI provider is healthy, which is exactly what happened on November 12.

## What this means for your workflow today

None of this is a reason to drop AI features that already save real hours on submittal prep, RFI drafting, or takeoff. It's a reason to stop treating them like email or your document repository. Keep anything time-critical, like a bid-day number or a same-day RFI response with contractual teeth, on a workflow a person can finish by hand. Check that overnight AI jobs actually ran before you rely on their output. Use the AI feature for the volume of work where a bad afternoon costs you some convenience, not a deadline.
