Hyperscaler capital spending is pacing toward $1.3 trillion in 2027, up from roughly $800 billion this year, according to Nvidia's own CFO on the company's August 26 earnings call. That's the demand side, and it hasn't slowed. What's changed since early summer is the friction sitting between that capital and a signed, buildable contract: Texas just froze grid approvals for a fifth of the entire national data center pipeline pending an audit, New York's permit freeze that was pending in July is now signed law, and insurers are openly saying they can't fully cover the biggest campuses. This is still a real, durable opportunity — it is a harder one to underwrite than it looked two months ago.

This guide is for the GC ops lead deciding whether to bid, and the electrical or mechanical sub owner deciding whether to invest in the certifications this work requires. It links out to our reporting on the specific pieces — state policy, equipment lead times, insurance capacity, workforce credentials — as they develop.

## How big is the data center construction boom, really?

US data center construction spending eclipsed $50 billion annually in 2026, a pace the market has never sustained before ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-01/us-construction-spending-on-data-centers-eclipses-50-billion)). The number behind that spending keeps moving up, not down: Nvidia CFO Colette Kress told analysts on August 26 that capital spending among the top five hyperscalers is on pace to hit roughly $1.3 trillion in 2027, up from about $800 billion in 2026 — a 62% jump in a single year ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/26/nvidia-nvda-earnings-report-q2-2027-live-updates.html)). That's higher than the $725 billion combined 2026 figure hyperscalers themselves confirmed in Q1 earnings, and higher than Moody's revised $785 billion forecast from June — the trend through the year has been upward revisions, not downward ones.

That capital keeps flowing into construction management fees, not just steel and concrete. ENR's 2026 Top 100 Professional Services Firms rankings show combined CM/PM-for-fee revenue hit $36.9 billion in 2025, up 31.2% from the year before, with the telecommunications and data center sector growing 31.3% and nearly 87% since 2023 ([ENR](https://www.enr.com/articles/63180-enr-2026-top-professional-services-firms-deepen-depth-of-services)). [Nvidia's own capex numbers and what they mean for a data center schedule are broken down here](/posts/2026-08-27-nvidia-earnings-hyperscaler-capex-data-center-schedule-risk).

## What's actually slowing data center projects down?

Equipment, mostly — and the lead times are getting more precise, not shorter. Manufacturer quotes as of late August put pad-mount distribution transformers at 40 to 65 weeks, substation transformers at 75 to 110 weeks, and generator step-up transformers over 50 MVA at 100 to 150-plus weeks. Standard switchgear runs about 52 weeks, but data-center-spec medium-voltage switchgear is approaching two to three years for some orders ([Terrapin Consulting Group](https://terrapincg.com/news/switchgear-transformer-generator-lead-times-2026)).

| Equipment | Typical lead time (late 2026) |
|---|---|
| Pad-mount distribution transformer | 40–65 weeks |
| Substation transformer (5–50 MVA) | 75–110 weeks |
| Generator step-up transformer (>50 MVA) | 100–150+ weeks |
| Standard switchgear/switchboard | ~52 weeks |
| Data-center-spec medium-voltage switchgear | Approaching 2–3 years |

Transformer manufacturers entered 2026 with order books already filled into 2028 and 2029, and Andreessen Horowitz put $1.1 billion behind a fund betting that gap doesn't close for years ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-28/andreessen-horowitz-raises-1-1-billion-for-ai-infrastructure-fund)). The practical shift this creates: the old sequence — finish design, then issue equipment purchase orders — doesn't survive these lead times. If a generator step-up transformer takes 150 weeks and your job has a 24-month schedule, that order has to go out before the electrical design is fully locked, against a preliminary load estimate the engineer refines later. ## Which states are open, and which just froze construction?

Power availability and permitting predictability, not incentive programs, are sorting the map — and the map moved twice since early July.

| Market | Status as of late August 2026 | What's driving it |
|---|---|---|
| **Texas** | Audit-paused for new interconnections | Aug. 3 order from Gov. Abbott directs ERCOT and the PUC to audit every project in the queue — about 250–300 projects, ~49.8 GW, a fifth of the national pipeline — before approving new ones; ERCOT targets completion by Dec. 10 ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ercot-texas-puc-data-center-audit/828472/)) |
| **New York** | Frozen for new permits | Executive Order No. 62, signed July 14, holds new state discretionary permits for data centers ≥50 MW in abeyance for up to a year; projects DEC had already deemed "complete" before that date are exempt ([Governor's office](https://www.governor.ny.gov/executive-order/no-62-establishing-temporary-moratorium-data-centers-new-york-while-state-develops)) |
| **Illinois** | Frozen for new entrants | Two-year suspension of state tax incentives, effective July 1, 2026 ([NRDC](https://www.nrdc.org/press-releases/pritzker-announces-two-year-suspension-state-tax-incentives-new-data-center)) |
| **Ohio, Georgia, Nevada** | Absorbing overflow demand | Cheaper power, available land, states actively courting projects; less mature subcontractor base means credential-building takes longer |
| **Northern Virginia (established)** | Mature but constrained | Deepest existing subcontractor base and utility infrastructure; highest construction cost per square foot in the country |

None of these freezes stop work already under construction or already permitted — the risk sits one step upstream, in the design-development and entitlement-phase work a firm may be carrying for a client's next phase. Texas's audit is the biggest single change: it was "wide open, growing fast" as recently as this spring, on track to overtake Northern Virginia as the world's top data center market by 2030 ([Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2026/02/texas-overtake-virginia-america-data-center-development-capital/)), and now roughly a fifth of the national pipeline is waiting on a state audit with a December target date and no guarantee every project clears it. ## What does data center electrical and mechanical work actually require?

More than a standard commercial scope. Hyperscaler facilities run at N+1 or 2N power redundancy — every major component has a hot standby, commissioned to switchover tolerances measured in milliseconds — and cooling has moved from conventional CRAC units to precision in-row cooling, rear-door heat exchangers, and direct liquid cooling manifolds for the highest-density GPU racks. Acceptance testing (integrated systems testing, or IST) simulates power and cooling failures on an energized facility with the construction team present, not a punch-list walkthrough after the fact. [The full qualification requirements — Tier III/IV project history, NFPA 70E documentation, DART safety floors, and the 18-to-24-month ramp to a first hyperscaler award — are broken down here](/posts/2026-06-30-data-center-electrical-credentials-725-billion), and [we cover what's required to qualify as an electrical sub for this work specifically here](/seo/how-to-qualify-as-electrical-sub-data-center-work).

## How much do data center construction jobs pay, and is the labor shortage real?

It's real, and AI companies are now spending directly to fix it rather than just outbidding for existing workers. Meta, Google, and BlackRock committed more than $265 million combined this year to train electricians and carpenters for AI data center work — Meta's $115 million America's Workforce Academy, Google.org's $50 million routed through IBEW/NECA's Electrical Training Alliance, and BlackRock's $100 million tied to its Texas buildout ([Construction Dive](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/meta-workforce-academy-data-center-construction/822698/)). Separately, OpenAI told the White House its own five-year infrastructure plans require roughly a fifth of the country's entire existing skilled-trades workforce — one company's build-out, not the industry total, layered on top of Google, Meta, Amazon, and everyone else competing for the same finite labor pool.

That's showing up in pay. Indeed Hiring Lab's July analysis found hourly installation and maintenance workers on data center projects earn about 42% more — roughly $10 an hour more — than workers in comparable roles outside data centers, with data center job postings more than tripling since May 2023 ([Indeed Hiring Lab](https://www.hiringlab.org/2026/07/14/hiring-for-the-data-center-build-out/)). Data center-specific electrician roles are commonly reported in the $72,000–$110,000 range, journeyman wireman packages at strong union locals clear $120,000-plus with overtime, and specialized roles in the hottest markets have been reported as high as $260,000 ([Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2026/04/05/us-data-centers-drive-explosion-in-pay-for-electricians-some-as-much-as-260-000/)). If your firm isn't building data centers but competes for electricians and carpenters in the same regional labor market — Northern Virginia and Dallas are the sharpest examples — that wage pressure and shortened bench reach your next bid whether or not a single data center is anywhere near your project. ## How much does it cost to build a data center in 2026?

It depends heavily on tier and region, but the range has widened as AI-density facilities pull the top end up. Enterprise Tier II space runs roughly $600–$750 per square foot, Tier III $750–$950, and Tier IV or hyperscale $950–$1,100-plus — while AI-optimized facilities with liquid cooling run $1,100–$1,500-plus per square foot ([Buildermuse](https://buildermuse.com/economy/data-center-construction-costs-per-square-foot/)). Shell construction is a comparatively modest $250–$400 per square foot; the mechanical, electrical, and cooling fit-out adds the other $800–$1,500. Northern Virginia remains the most expensive market in the country, driven by labor competition and utility connection fees, while Ohio and Phoenix run 15–20% cheaper on labor alone.

## Can a GC actually insure a data center project at full value?

Often, no. AIG CEO Eric Andersen said in an August 11 interview that the AI data center buildout is "absolutely maxing out the P&C insurance industry in terms of the limits that are required," noting a single project needs roughly 30 different insurance products spanning project finance, construction, cyber, property, and liability ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/ai-data-center-boom-is-maxing-out-p-c-insurers-aig-ceo-says)). Data center campuses that ran $1 billion to $2.5 billion two years ago are now routinely $5 billion to $25 billion — and the largest dedicated builders risk program in the market, Aon's Data Center Lifecycle Insurance Program, tops out at $3.5 billion in construction-all-risk capacity after two expansions this year ([Risk & Insurance](https://riskandinsurance.com/data-center-insurance-capacity-strained-as-average-project-values-surge-from-150-million-to-3-billion/)). Many projects are running with policy limits covering only a third to a half of total value — and that uncovered gap doesn't stay the owner's problem by default. On a CM-at-risk or design-build contract, it often lands partly on the GC, depending on how the risk-allocation and subrogation-waiver language is written. Get the builders risk placement quoted during precon, not after award, and get the coverage gap allocated in the contract rather than assumed. [The full breakdown of who's exposed and what to check before bidding is here](/posts/2026-08-13-aig-data-center-insurance-capacity-builders-risk-gap).

There's a financing-side counterpart worth checking too: the largest data center operators are now backing their utility payment obligations with billions in bank-guaranteed letters of credit — QTS with about $2 billion, Switch with close to $10 billion combined — specifically to convince utilities to start building the substations a campus needs before the operator has a track record. A developer with that financing in place is a meaningfully more reliable client than one with just a signed land lease and a press release. [What a bank guarantee tells you about whether a project's power (and schedule) is actually real is here](/posts/2026-08-05-data-center-bank-guarantees-power-queue-gc-schedule).

## Is the data center boom a bubble that could leave contractors overextended?

The demand side doesn't support that read — capex commitments are rising, not falling, and Nvidia's own numbers came in higher than Moody's June forecast rather than lower. The real risk is narrower and more specific to construction: facilities are designed to stand for 25 to 30 years, and the electrical and cooling infrastructure going in today is sized for 2026 GPU power density, which could look oversized if compute efficiency improves faster than expected.

There's also a newer wrinkle: Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said on the same August earnings call that the company has "supply for 70% growth" in chip output next year but that "our demand is much higher than that" — meaning Nvidia's own factories, not customer appetite, are now the ceiling on how fast the buildout moves. For a GC, that inverts the usual risk picture. A building can be finished and power energized, and the project still waits on IT-load energization because the owner's GPU order hasn't shipped — a delay with nothing to do with construction quality. Decouple shell/core substantial completion from IT-load energization in the contract where you can, so a chip delay doesn't read as a construction delay.

The more immediate commercial risk: projects needing new transmission infrastructure now run five to eight years from decision to energization, up from one to three years before the pandemic — schedule risk that shows up as cash-flow and bonding exposure long before any compute bubble would ([SWK Technologies](https://www.swktech.com/data-center-projects-forecasting-risk/)). Peak crews on the largest campuses run 4,000 to 5,000 workers, a management and logistics scale most GCs haven't handled before.

## How do you get on a hyperscaler's preferred contractor list?

It's a documentation exercise more than a bid exercise. Hyperscaler procurement teams typically screen for prior Uptime Institute Tier III or higher project experience, written NFPA 70E arc flash hazard analysis and PPE programs for every technician, DART safety rates below program-specific floors, and — for some federal or defense-adjacent workloads — a background-checked or ITAR-cleared workforce. Firms that already have this in place made the investment around 2024 and are largely booked. For everyone else, the realistic path is 18 to 24 months: build the credential trail as a second-tier sub under a qualified GC, then bid direct once the documentation and project history exist.

## The honest read

This is not a market you drift into on a slow quarter, and it's gotten harder to underwrite in the two months since we first covered it. The capital is real and still accelerating — $1.3 trillion in 2027 isn't a soft number — but the constraints stacking up around it aren't just equipment and crews anymore. A state can now freeze a fifth of the national pipeline with one audit order, and a $20 billion campus may not be fully insurable at any price. Firms winning this work today mostly made the decision to pursue it two years ago, before any of that friction existed. If your shop has clean safety records, capacity in electrical or mechanical disciplines, and the patience for an 18-to-24-month credential ramp plus the contract discipline to negotiate insurance gaps and chip-supply schedule risk explicitly, the arithmetic still favors starting now. If you're evaluating this quarter-to-quarter looking for fast backlog, this is the wrong market — the paperwork, qualification runway, and now regulatory whiplash will outlast most short-term sourcing decisions. For the paperwork itself, once you're on a data center job, [AI is already changing how RFIs, submittals, and change orders move through the log](/seo/ai-construction-rfi-submittal-change-order-guide) — worth understanding before your first big campus award, not after.
